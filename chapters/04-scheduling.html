9  Project Scheduling & Critical Path Method

Chapter 4 · Planning

Project Scheduling & Critical Path Method

From WBS to timeline: activity sequencing, Gantt charts, network diagrams, CPM, and PERT analysis.

Learning Outcomes

Tip🎯 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Explain why time management is the most challenging project constraint and how individual/cultural differences affect scheduling
  2. Describe the Schedule Management Plan and its key components
  3. Define activities from a WBS, document an Activity List, and distinguish effort from duration
  4. Classify dependencies as mandatory, discretionary, or external, and apply FS/SS/FF/SF relationships
  5. Estimate activity resources using a Resource Breakdown Structure; apply Brooks’ Law to resource decisions
  6. Sequence activities and draw an Activity on Node (AON) network diagram
  7. Calculate the Critical Path using forward and backward pass; compute total float and free float
  8. Develop a Gantt chart with SMART milestones and a tracking Gantt for schedule control
  9. Apply PERT three-point estimation to quantify schedule uncertainty
  10. Explain schedule compression techniques (crashing, fast-tracking) and critical chain scheduling
  11. Control the schedule using variance analysis, tracking Gantts, and best-practice reality checks

9.1 The Scheduling Process 📋 Required

9.2 Why Time Management Matters 📋 Required

Time management is frequently cited as the most challenging dimension of the triple constraint. Schedule issues are the primary source of conflict on IT and engineering projects — ahead of budget disputes or scope changes.

Warning⚠️ The Schedule Challenge
  • Time is the least flexible constraint: You can find extra budget; you can negotiate scope — but you cannot recover lost calendar time.
  • Schedule overruns are visible: Clients see delays immediately; cost overruns can be hidden longer.
  • IT projects are persistently late: Studies show the average IT project overruns its schedule by 70%; the Standish Group CHAOS Report consistently identifies schedule as the top failure dimension.

9.2.1 Individual and Cultural Differences

People’s relationship with time varies — this affects how teams approach schedules:

Dimension Description Scheduling Implication
Structured (J-type) — Myers-Briggs Plans ahead, wants defined milestones, dislikes uncertainty Follows schedules well; may over-engineer early planning
Flexible (P-type) — Myers-Briggs Adapts as they go, resists rigid timelines, works under pressure Tends to use up float; milestone reminders are essential
Monochronic cultures Sequential focus; punctuality is a value; time is a resource Will adhere to schedules; expect dates to be honoured
Polychronic cultures Multiple simultaneous activities; relationships over deadlines Need clearer milestone expectations and follow-up

Awareness of these differences helps PMs design realistic schedules and communicate deadlines effectively.

9.3 Planning Schedule Management 📋 Required

Before defining any activities, the project team must agree on how scheduling will be done. This is captured in the Schedule Management Plan — a subsidiary plan within the overall Project Management Plan.

Note📖 Schedule Management Plan — Contents
Component What It Defines
Scheduling methodology CPM, critical chain, agile sprints, or a combination; which software tool
Level of accuracy How precise must estimates be? (±10%, nearest hour, nearest day)
Units of measure Hours, days, weeks, or calendar dates — consistent across all activities
Control thresholds Acceptable variance before action is required (e.g., ±5% schedule variance)
Performance measurement rules How progress is measured: % complete, milestone-based, or earned value
Reporting formats What schedule reports go to whom, and how often (weekly Gantt to PM; monthly S-curve to sponsor)
Process descriptions Who updates the schedule? Who approves changes? What is the change control process?

The schedule management plan is approved early in the project and defines the rules for all subsequent scheduling work.

9.4 Activity Definition and Duration Estimating 📋 Required

9.4.1 From Work Packages to Activities

Work packages from the WBS must be further broken down into schedule activities — the individual tasks that can be tracked on a daily/weekly basis.

WBS Work Package Schedule Activities
1.3.1 Foundation Design A1: Geotechnical data review; A2: Foundation type analysis; A3: Structural calculations; A4: Foundation drawings; A5: Specification writing
1.4.2 Concrete Structures B1: Formwork fabrication; B2: Reinforcement fixing; B3: Concrete pouring; B4: Curing; B5: Formwork striking; B6: Surface finishing

9.4.2 Activity List and Activity Attributes

The Activity List is a formal project document listing every schedule activity with its key attributes. Each entry contains enough information for planning and tracking:

Attribute Description
Activity ID Unique identifier (links back to WBS)
Activity Name Short descriptive label
Activity Description What work is being performed
Predecessor Activities Which activities must finish/start before this one begins
Successor Activities Which activities depend on this one
Leads and Lags Any time offsets applied to dependencies
Resource Requirements People, equipment, and materials needed
Constraints Fixed dates, imposed deadlines, or resource restrictions
Assumptions What is assumed to be true (affects duration and cost)
Tip💡 Who Should Estimate?

The people who will actually do the work should provide duration estimates — not the project manager alone. They have the most accurate knowledge of the effort involved, the risks, and the constraints. The PM’s role is to integrate estimates and challenge unrealistic ones.

9.4.3 Duration Estimation Methods

  • Analogous Estimating
  • Parametric Estimating
  • Three-Point (PERT) Estimating
  • Bottom-Up Estimating

Use historical data from similar projects

  • Fast and low effort
  • Less accurate
  • Best for early-stage estimates

Example: “Our last 50MW substation took 18 months, so this 60MW substation will take about 21 months”

Accuracy: ±25% to ±50%

Use statistical relationships between variables

  • Uses unit rates or formulas
  • More accurate than analogous
  • Requires reliable data

Example: “Excavation rate for this soil type is 120m³/day with a 20-tonne excavator. We have 2,400m³ to excavate, so: 2,400 ÷ 120 = 20 days”

Accuracy: ±10% to ±25%

Use optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic estimates

Three estimates for each activity: - Optimistic (O): Best case, everything goes right - Most Likely (M): Most realistic estimate - Pessimistic (P): Worst case, everything goes wrong

PERT Expected Duration: \[t_E = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6}\]

PERT Standard Deviation: \[\sigma = \frac{P - O}{6}\]

PERT Variance: \[\sigma^2 = \left(\frac{P - O}{6}\right)^2\]

Estimate each activity individually, then aggregate

  • Most accurate method
  • Most time-consuming
  • Best for detailed planning

Process: 1. Identify all activities 2. Estimate each individually with subject matter experts 3. Sum all activities for total project duration 4. Add buffer/contingency

Accuracy: ±5% to ±15%

9.5 Effort, Duration, and Elapsed Time

These three terms are often confused but have distinct meanings:

Term Definition Example
Effort The total amount of work required (person-hours) A task requires 40 person-hours of work
Duration The calendar time from start to finish That same task takes 5 working days (8h/day × 5 = 40h)
Elapsed time Calendar time including weekends, holidays, waiting Concrete curing takes 7 elapsed days even if no one works
Note📖 Key Distinctions
  • A task that needs 40 hours of effort by one person at 8h/day = 5 working days of duration
  • The same task with two people = still 40 hours of effort, but ~2.5 days of duration (with coordination overhead)
  • A physical process like paint drying adds elapsed time (duration) with zero additional effort

Beware the common mistake of treating effort = duration. A PM who assigns two people to a 40-hour task and shows 2.5 days in the schedule must verify that the task is actually parallelisable — not all work can be divided.

9.6 Estimating Activity Resources 📋 Required

Before finalising duration estimates, the project manager must determine what resources are needed for each activity. Resources include:

  • Human resources — specific people, roles, or skill sets
  • Equipment — machinery, vehicles, test equipment, lab facilities
  • Materials — hardware, raw materials, software licences

9.6.1 Resource Breakdown Structure (RBS)

Just as the WBS breaks down the scope, the Resource Breakdown Structure (RBS) categorises all project resources hierarchically. It helps identify potential resource conflicts and ensures every activity has its resource needs documented.

9.6.2 The Resource-Duration Chicken-and-Egg Problem

Resource estimation and duration estimation are interdependent — which makes scheduling an iterative process:

  • Duration depends on how many resources are assigned
  • Resource availability depends on the schedule

The practical approach: estimate duration assuming a standard resource assignment, then level the resources (resolve over-allocations) afterwards.

9.6.3 Mythical Man-Month — Brooks’ Law

Warning⚠️ Adding People to a Late Software Project Makes It Later

Frederick Brooks in “The Mythical Man-Month” (1975) identified a counter-intuitive truth about software projects:

“Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.”

Why? Because: 1. New team members need ramp-up time (they consume effort from existing members) 2. Communication overhead increases with each additional person: \(n(n-1)/2\) communication channels for \(n\) people 3. Many software tasks are sequential, not parallel — a woman can have one baby in 9 months; nine women cannot have one baby in one month

Implication: Do not reflexively crash a software project by adding people. Better to focus existing team, reduce scope, or extend the deadline.

9.6.4 Resource Leveling

When the initial schedule creates resource over-allocations (a person assigned to more work than available hours allow), resource leveling resolves conflicts by:

  • Delaying non-critical activities within their available float
  • Shifting resources from non-critical to critical tasks
  • Extending the schedule when float is insufficient

In MS Project: Resource tab → Level Resources applies automatic leveling. The schedule end date will typically move later. The PM must then negotiate whether this is acceptable or whether additional resources must be sourced.

9.7 Activity Sequencing — Dependency Types 📋 Required

Activities must be sequenced based on their logical dependencies:

Finish-to-Start (FS) Most Common
Activity A
⟶
Activity B
B cannot start until A finishes
e.g. Excavation must finish before concrete pour starts
Start-to-Start (SS)
Activity A
⟶
Activity B
B cannot start until A starts
e.g. Training can begin once documentation starts
Finish-to-Finish (FF)
Activity A
⟶
Activity B
B cannot finish until A finishes
e.g. QA testing cannot end until development ends
Start-to-Finish (SF) Rare
Activity A
⟶
Activity B
B cannot finish until A starts
e.g. New system (A) starts → old system (B) can shut down

9.7.1 Lag and Lead Time

  • Lag: A delay after a dependency is satisfied
    • Example: Concrete curing — next activity starts 7 days after pouring finishes (FS + 7 days lag)
  • Lead: An overlap — the successor activity starts before the predecessor finishes
    • Example: Begin procurement drawings while detailed design is still 80% complete (FS - 2 weeks lead)

9.7.2 Types of Dependencies

Beyond the four relationship types (FS/SS/FF/SF), dependencies are also classified by their source:

Type Also Called Definition Example
Mandatory Hard logic Inherent in the nature of the work — cannot be changed Foundation must cure before walls are built (physics)
Discretionary Soft logic, preferred logic Based on best practice or preference — can be changed if needed PMs prefer to complete testing before documentation, but could overlap
External — Depends on a party outside the project team Regulatory approval must be received before construction begins
Warning⚠️ Be Careful with Discretionary Dependencies

Discretionary (soft) dependencies are often added out of habit or preference. They reduce scheduling flexibility — and under schedule pressure, teams may break them anyway (creating risk). Review all soft logic before baselineing the schedule. Only keep discretionary dependencies that are genuinely important.

9.8 Network Diagrams — Activity on Arrow (AOA) 📋 Required

The Activity on Arrow (AOA) method — also called the Arrow Diagramming Method (ADM) — places activities on arrows and uses numbered circles (nodes) to represent events (start/finish milestones):

Note📖 AOA Rules
Feature Description
Activities Represented by arrows (→); label shows the activity name and duration
Events / Nodes Numbered circles (①②③…) at the tail (start) and head (finish) of each arrow
Node rule An activity can only start after all activities arriving at its tail node are complete
Dummy Activities Dashed arrows (- - ▶) with zero duration; used to show logic that cannot be expressed directly
Dependency type Finish-to-Start (FS) only — AOA cannot represent SS, FF, or SF lags
Identification Each activity is uniquely identified by its i-j node pair (e.g., activity 1-2)
Usage today Less common than AON; still used in civil engineering, construction, and CPM/PERT exams

9.8.1 AOA Example — Software Release Project

We will use this five-activity software release as the running example for both AOA and AON (and the conversion between them):

Activity Description Predecessor(s) Duration
A Gather Requirements — 3 days
B Create UI Mockups — 2 days
C Develop Features A and B 5 days
D Write User Manual B only 2 days
E System Testing C 3 days
Tip🔑 Key Logic to Represent
  • C needs both A and B before it can start
  • D needs only B — it does NOT need A
  • This split dependency is the classic situation requiring a dummy activity in AOA

Before tackling the split dependency, here is a simple purely sequential AOA to show the diagram conventions:

Activity Description Predecessor Duration
A Survey and set out — 2 days
B Excavation and subgrade A 5 days
C Sub-base compaction B 3 days
D Base course laying C 4 days
E Surface course and line marking D 2 days

9.8.3 AOA Dummy Activities

A dummy activity is a dashed arrow with zero duration and zero cost. It represents a logical dependency only — it adds no real work.

Warning⚠️ Two Reasons You Need a Dummy

Reason 1 — Uniqueness constraint: Two real activities share the exact same start node and end node. In AOA every activity is identified by its i-j pair. If activity A and activity B both run from node ① to node ②, they are indistinguishable. A dummy gives one of them a unique node.

Reason 2 — Logic constraint: You need to express that activity C depends on both A and B, while activity D depends only on B — not on A. You cannot draw this correctly in AOA without a dummy.

Reason 1 — Uniqueness: Two Parallel Activities

Given activities:

Activity Description Predecessor(s) Duration
A Design UI — 3 days
B Write Specs — 2 days
C Develop Module A and B 5 days

A and B both start independently and both must finish before C can begin. Because A and B share the same start node AND would share the same end node, they cannot be uniquely identified in AOA — a dummy is required.

Wrong (ambiguous — both are “activity 1-2”):

Correct (dummy gives B a unique i-j pair):

Node ② now receives: A (from ①→②) AND the dummy (from ③→②, carrying B’s completion). So C waits for both A and B — correct logic, and every activity has a unique i-j identifier.

Reason 2 — Logic: Split Dependency (Software Release Example)

Given activities:

Activity Description Predecessor(s) Duration
A Gather Requirements — 3 days
B Create UI Mockups — 2 days
C Develop Features A and B 5 days
D Write User Manual B only 2 days
E System Testing C 3 days

The critical dependency: C needs both A and B, but D needs B only. Drawing D and C both starting from the same node would force D to also wait for A — a false dependency. A dummy is required to correctly isolate the split.

Requirement: C needs A and B; D needs B only.

Step-by-step AOA construction:

  1. Since D needs only B, B must end at a node where only B arrives — that becomes D’s start node.
  2. Since C needs both A and B, C must start from a node where both arrive.
  3. A already ends at its own node. Add a dummy from B’s end-node to A’s end-node to carry B’s completion into C’s prerequisite check.

Tip✅ Verify the Logic
Activity Starts from What flows into that node Correct?
D Node ② B only (①→②) ✅ D needs only B
C Node ③ A (①→③) + Dummy (②→③, carrying B) ✅ C needs A and B
E Node ④ C (③→④) ✅ E needs only C

The dummy arrow ②→③ is the key: it carries B’s completion into node ③ without creating a false dependency between A and D.

Important❌ Common Mistake — Wrong Dummy Direction

If you draw the dummy as ②→③ where ② is A’s end and ③ is B’s end, then D (starting from ③) would also inherit A — giving D a false dependency on A. Always ensure D’s start node has only its required predecessors flowing into it.

9.8.4 AOA Summary Diagram (All 5 Activities)

Reading the diagram: - Activity B (2d) and Activity A (3d) start simultaneously from node ①
- D starts as soon as B finishes (node ②), independently of A
- C can only start after both A finishes (reaching ③ directly) and B finishes (reaching ③ via the dummy)
- E follows C; the project ends when both E and D are complete at node ⑥

9.9 Network Diagrams — Activity on Node (AON) 📋 Required

The Activity on Node (AON) method — also called the Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM) — places activities inside boxes (nodes) and uses arrows to show dependencies:

Note📖 AON Rules
Feature Description
Activities Represented by boxes/rectangles; contain name, ID, and duration
Arrows Show dependencies only — the arrow means “must finish before” (for FS)
Dependency types FS, SS, FF, SF — all four types supported; lags and leads can be shown
Dummy activities Never needed — every dependency is an explicit arrow
Start / End nodes Optional “Start” and “End” milestone nodes used to clearly bound the network
Used by All modern PM software: MS Project, Primavera P6, Oracle, etc.

9.9.1 AON Example — Same Software Release Project

Using identical activities as the AOA example above, notice how the split dependency is expressed cleanly — no dummy required:

Activity Description Predecessor(s) Duration
A Gather Requirements — 3 days
B Create UI Mockups — 2 days
C Develop Features A and B 5 days
D Write User Manual B only 2 days
E System Testing C 3 days

Tip✅ Verify the Logic
Activity Incoming arrows Correct?
C from A and from B ✅ C needs A and B
D from B only ✅ D needs only B
E from C only ✅ E needs only C

In AON, D simply receives one arrow from B — there is no need for a dummy to isolate the dependency.

9.9.3 AOA vs. AON — Side-by-Side Comparison

Feature AOA (Arrow Diagramming) AON (Precedence Diagramming)
Activities represented as Arrows Boxes (nodes)
Nodes/circles represent Events / milestones Activities themselves
Dependencies shown by Numbered node pairs (i-j) Explicit arrows between boxes

In practice, AON is the standard for all modern project scheduling software. AOA is still examined in PMP/CAPM certification exams and appears in older construction scheduling literature.

9.10 Converting Between AOA and AON 📋 Required

The same network can always be expressed in both notations. The Software Release example above will be used to demonstrate both directions.

9.10.1 Converting AOA → AON

Note📖 AOA → AON Conversion Rules
  1. Each real activity arrow in AOA becomes a box (node) in AON.
  2. Each dummy activity is discarded — it carries no real work.
  3. For every two activities X and Y in AOA where X’s end-node is Y’s start-node (directly or via a dummy chain), draw an arrow from X’s box to Y’s box in AON.
  4. If multiple activities feed into the same start node in AOA, all of them become incoming arrows to the same box in AON.
  5. Add a START and END milestone box to bound the network.

Step-by-step conversion of the Software Release AOA → AON:

Step Action
1 List all real activities: A, B, C, D, E
2 Identify the dummy: ②→③. Discard it, but remember what it implied: node ③ is reached by both A (directly) and B (via dummy from ②). So B is a predecessor of C (just like A).
3 Read predecessors from the AOA node logic: D starts from ② (only B); C starts from ③ (A and B); E starts from ④ (only C)
4 Draw boxes for A, B, C, D, E and connect with arrows

9.10.2 Converting AON → AOA

Note📖 AON → AOA Conversion Rules
  1. Each activity box in AON becomes an arrow in AOA.
  2. Assign numbered nodes (circles) at the start and end of each arrow.
  3. If two or more activities share the same set of predecessors and the same set of successors, they may share start and/or end nodes — but only if this causes no false dependencies. If sharing a node would force an unintended dependency, add a dummy activity.
  4. When activity Y has predecessors X₁ and X₂ in AON: X₁ and X₂ must both end at Y’s start node in AOA. If X₁ and X₂ already end at different nodes, add a dummy from one of those nodes to the other (or to a new merge node).
  5. Check the uniqueness rule: no two real activities may share both the same start node and the same end node.

Step-by-step conversion of the Software Release AON → AOA:

Step What we know from AON AOA action
1 A: no predecessor, successor = C Arrow A from a new start node ①
2 B: no predecessor, successors = C and D Arrow B also from ①. B and A share the same start node (① — fine, different end nodes).
3 D: predecessor = B only D can start from B’s end node. Let ② = B’s end. D: ②→⑤
4 C: predecessors = A and B C must start from a node where both A and B are complete. A ends at ③. B ends at ②. Add Dummy ②→③ to carry B’s completion into ③. C: ③→④
5 E: predecessor = C only E: ④→⑥
6 End: all paths converge at ⑥ D: ②→⑤→⑥ (add a final merge, or let ⑤ = ⑥ if D and E both end the project)

Tip💡 Key Insight — Why the Dummy Appears in AOA but Not AON

In AON, you simply draw two separate arrows into C (one from A, one from B) — no special mechanism needed.

In AOA, a node is a shared milestone. For C to need both A and B, both must arrive at the same node. But D must not see A — so D’s start node can only have B flowing in. The dummy is the only way to “route” B’s completion into the A+B merge node without forcing D to also wait for A.

The dummy solves the structural limitation that AOA can only merge dependencies at a single shared node.

9.11 Critical Path Method (CPM) 📋 Required

The Critical Path Method (CPM) identifies the longest path through the network — the sequence of activities that determines the minimum project duration.

Note📖 CPM Basic Terms & Formulas
Term Formula Meaning
ES — Early Start \(ES = \max(EF \text{ of predecessors})\) Earliest an activity can start
EF — Early Finish \(EF = ES + Duration\) Earliest an activity can finish
LF — Late Finish \(LF = \min(LS \text{ of successors})\) Latest it can finish without delaying the project
LS — Late Start \(LS = LF - Duration\) Latest it can start without delaying the project
Total Float \(Float = LS - ES \;=\; LF - EF\) Flexibility an activity has before it delays the project
Free Float \(FF = ES_{successor} - EF\) Flexibility before delaying the next activity
Critical Path Activities where \(Float = 0\) Longest path — determines total project duration
Critical Activity \(Float = 0\) Any delay on this activity delays the entire project

9.11.1 Step-by-Step CPM Calculation

Example Network:

Activity Duration (days) Predecessors
A 3 —
B 5 A
C 4 A
D 6 B
E 3 B, C
F 4 D, E

Step 1: Forward Pass (Calculate ES and EF)

Tip💡 Starting Convention

The first activity always has ES = 0 by convention — the project clock starts at zero. The formula \(ES = \max(EF \text{ of predecessors})\) only applies to activities that have predecessors. Similarly, in the backward pass, LF of the last activity = project duration (not calculated from successors).

\[ES_A = 0 \quad \text{(by convention — no predecessors)}, \quad EF_A = ES_A + Duration_A = 0 + 3 = 3\]

\[ES_B = EF_A = 3, \quad EF_B = 3 + 5 = 8\]

\[ES_C = EF_A = 3, \quad EF_C = 3 + 4 = 7\]

\[ES_D = EF_B = 8, \quad EF_D = 8 + 6 = 14\]

\[ES_E = \max(EF_B, EF_C) = \max(8, 7) = 8, \quad EF_E = 8 + 3 = 11\]

\[ES_F = \max(EF_D, EF_E) = \max(14, 11) = 14, \quad EF_F = 14 + 4 = 18\]

Project Duration = 18 days

Step 2: Backward Pass (Calculate LS and LF)

9.13 PERT Analysis — Handling Schedule Uncertainty 📋 Required

PERT (Programme Evaluation and Review Technique) extends CPM by acknowledging that activity durations are uncertain and modelling them probabilistically.

9.13.1 PERT Three-Point Estimating

For each activity, obtain three estimates and apply the PERT formulas:

\[t_E = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6} \qquad \sigma = \frac{P - O}{6} \qquad \sigma^2 = \left(\frac{P-O}{6}\right)^2\]

Where: - O (Optimistic) — best-case duration if everything goes perfectly - M (Most Likely) — the most realistic, expected duration under normal conditions - P (Pessimistic) — worst-case duration if most things go wrong - \(t_E\) (Expected Duration) — the weighted average; M is weighted 4× because it is the most likely outcome - \(\sigma\) (Standard Deviation) — how spread out the estimate is; large σ means high uncertainty - \(\sigma^2\) (Variance) — used when combining uncertainty across multiple activities on the critical path (variances add, standard deviations do not)

Worked calculations:

PERT three-point estimates with full calculation steps
Activity O M P \(O + 4M + P\) \(t_E = \div 6\) \(P - O\) \(\sigma = \div 6\) \(\sigma^2\)
Foundation Design 8 12 22 \(8+48+22=78\) 13.00 \(22-8=14\) 2.33 5.44
Structural Design 12 18 30 \(12+72+30=114\) 19.00 \(30-12=18\) 3.00 9.00
Drawing Production 6 9 15 \(6+36+15=57\) 9.50 \(15-6=9\) 1.50 2.25
Approvals 14 21 56 \(14+84+56=154\) 25.67 \(56-14=42\) 7.00 49.00
Tip💡 Reading the Table
  • Large gap between O and P (e.g., Approvals: 14 vs 56) → high uncertainty → large σ (7.00) → this activity dominates the project’s schedule risk
  • Small gap (e.g., Drawing Production: 6 vs 15) → lower uncertainty → σ = 1.50 → more predictable
  • \(t_E\) is always pulled toward P when P is far from M — the pessimistic tail has real weight
  • Add variances (not σ) when computing the overall project standard deviation: \(\sigma_{project} = \sqrt{\sum \sigma^2}\)
Note💡 ملاحظة

نشاط “الموافقات” له أكبر فجوة بين O و P (14 مقابل 56)، مما يعني عدم يقين عالٍ. هذا النشاط هو المصدر الرئيسي لمخاطر الجدول الزمني.

Project Expected Duration — sum all \(t_E\) values on the critical path:

\[T_E = 13.00 + 19.00 + 9.50 + 25.67 = \mathbf{67.17 \text{ days}}\]

Project Standard Deviation — sum the variances (not σ), then take the square root:

\[\sigma_{project} = \sqrt{\sigma^2_1 + \sigma^2_2 + \sigma^2_3 + \sigma^2_4} = \sqrt{5.44 + 9.00 + 2.25 + 49.00} = \sqrt{65.69} = \mathbf{8.11 \text{ days}}\]

Warning⚠️ Why Add Variances, Not Standard Deviations?

You cannot add σ values directly: \(2.33 + 3.00 + 1.50 + 7.00 = 13.83\) — this overstates uncertainty.

Variances add correctly because they measure independent squared spread. You take the square root after summing to get back to days: \[\sigma_{project} = \sqrt{\sum \sigma^2} <br/>eq \sum \sigma\]

9.13.2 Probability of Completion by Target Date

Using the normal distribution:

\[Z = \frac{T_{target} - T_{expected}}{\sigma_{project}}\]

Where \(T_{target}\) is the desired completion date and \(T_{expected}\) is the calculated expected completion.

The Z-score tells you how many standard deviations the target date is above the expected duration. You then look up that Z-score in the Standard Normal Distribution Table below to find the probability:

9.14 Schedule Compression Techniques 📋 Required

When the calculated schedule exceeds the target date, two primary compression techniques are available:

💰 CRASHING
  • Add more resources to critical path activities
  • Increases cost; may increase risk
  • Use cost-slope to find cheapest compression per day
  • Always crash the lowest cost-slope activity first
⚡ FAST-TRACKING
  • Run sequential activities in parallel (using lead times)
  • Increases risk of rework if earlier work changes
  • No direct additional cost
  • Requires close coordination and clear interfaces

9.14.1 Cost-Slope for Crashing

The cost-slope measures how much it costs to shorten an activity by one unit of time (one day). It lets you compare activities and always crash the cheapest one first.

\[\text{Cost Slope} = \frac{C_c - C_n}{T_n - T_c}\]

Symbol Meaning
\(C_n\) Normal Cost — cost under the original schedule
\(C_c\) Crash Cost — cost when the activity is accelerated
\(T_n\) Normal Duration — original planned duration
\(T_c\) Crash Duration — shortest achievable duration

Worked calculations for each activity:

B 8d 6d $8,000 $12,000 $4,000 2 days $2,000/day 🥇 1st

Tip💡 The Golden Rule of Crashing

Always crash the critical path activity with the lowest cost-slope first, then re-analyse.

Only critical path activities matter — crashing a non-critical activity saves zero project time.

Goal Best choice
Save cost Crash A or B ($2,000/day)
Save maximum time Crash C (4 days available)
Balanced — save 2 days cheapest Crash A or B: 2 × $2,000 = $4,000 vs C or D: 2 × $3,000 = $6,000

Step-by-step crashing process:

  1. Identify activities on the critical path only
  2. Calculate cost-slope for each critical activity
  3. Sort ascending by cost-slope (cheapest first)
  4. Crash the cheapest activity — but do not exceed its maximum crash limit (\(T_n - T_c\))
  5. Re-analyse the critical path — it may shift after crashing
  6. Repeat until the target duration is reached

Quick example — save 3 days at minimum cost:

Activity A has a crash limit of 3 days (10 → 7) at $2,000/day:

\[\text{Additional cost} = 3 \times \$2{,}000 = \mathbf{\$6{,}000}\]

Alternatively, use Activity B (limit: 2 days) + Activity C (1 day):

\[2 \times \$2{,}000 + 1 \times \$3{,}000 = \$4{,}000 + \$3{,}000 = \mathbf{\$7{,}000}\]

9.15 Critical Chain Scheduling 📋 Required

Critical Chain Scheduling is an alternative to CPM developed by Eliyahu Goldratt based on his Theory of Constraints (from the novel “The Goal”, 1984). It focuses on managing buffers rather than individual activity floats.

9.15.1 The Problem with Traditional Scheduling

When PMs ask team members to estimate task durations, team members build in individual safety buffers — often doubling the realistic estimate. This leads to two dysfunctions:

Warning⚠️ Parkinson’s Law + Murphy’s Law = Schedule Disaster
  • Parkinson’s Law: “Work expands to fill the time available.” If an activity has 10 days in the schedule, it will take 10 days — even if it could be done in 6. People do not finish early; they use the buffer for other work or polishing.
  • Murphy’s Law: “Anything that can go wrong, will go wrong.” Problems arise anyway, consuming the individual buffers — and the project still runs late.

The result: individual padding doesn’t protect the project end date, but it does inflate the schedule and slow down the team.

9.15.2 The Multitasking Problem

Critical chain scheduling addresses the harm caused by excessive multitasking. Consider a team member assigned to three parallel tasks (A, B, C), each requiring 10 working days:

Approach Task A finishes Task B finishes Task C finishes
Serial (one at a time) Day 10 Day 20 Day 30
Parallel multitasking (one third each day) Day 30 Day 30 Day 30

Parallel multitasking delays every task and adds context-switching overhead. The solution: focus — finish one task, then start the next.

9.15.3 How Critical Chain Scheduling Works

Steps in critical chain scheduling:

  1. Remove individual safety buffers from activity estimates — use the 50% likely duration (not the padded estimate)
  2. Identify the critical chain — the longest chain of dependent activities considering resource constraints (not just logic links)
  3. Add a Project Buffer at the end of the critical chain before the project due date — this is where the removed padding goes
  4. Add Feeding Buffers where non-critical chains feed into the critical chain — to protect the critical chain from delays on feeder paths
  5. Manage the buffer — track what percentage of the buffer has been consumed vs. how much of the project is complete

9.15.4 Three Organisational Approaches to Buffers

Organisation Type Behaviour Outcome
Hard-nose No buffers allowed; pressures people to commit to minimum durations Team members hide buffers in their own work; no visibility
Lenient Allows individual buffers in each task Parkinson’s Law applies; project still runs late
Critical chain Removes individual buffers; PM controls an aggregate project buffer Buffer is visible; PM can see when project is truly at risk
Tip💡 Why Not Show the Buffer to the Team?

The project buffer should be managed by the PM, not consumed by the team. If team members know there is a 15-day buffer, they will use it. The buffer is a management reserve — not extra time for individual tasks.

9.16 Agile Time Management ➕ Extended

Traditional CPM scheduling assumes all work is defined upfront — you know every activity before the project starts. Agile projects take a fundamentally different approach.

Note📖 Agile Scheduling Principles
  • Don’t define all work before scheduling: Only plan in detail what will be done in the next sprint (1–4 weeks)
  • Product Backlog: A prioritised list of all desired features and work — managed by the Product Owner
  • Sprint Backlog: The subset of backlog items the team commits to completing in the current sprint
  • Velocity: The team’s average output per sprint (in story points or hours), used to predict future delivery
  • Responding to change over following a plan: The schedule adapts sprint-by-sprint based on what has been learned and what stakeholders now want

  1. Know the current status of the schedule — what is on time, what is behind
  2. Identify the factors causing schedule changes before they become delays
  3. Determine whether the schedule has changed and by how much
  4. Manage the actual changes when they occur

9.17.2 Reality Checks and Best Practices

Tip💡 Schedule Control — Reality Checks

  1. Do not plan everyone at 100% capacity. Real work includes email, meetings, administrative tasks, and unexpected interruptions. Planning at 80–85% capacity is more realistic.

  2. Hold regular progress meetings with the team and stakeholders. Not to report blame, but to surface problems early when there is still time to respond.

  3. Be honest with your sponsor when the schedule is slipping. Hiding a delay is worse than surfacing it early — sponsors can help (more resources, scope reduction, deadline extension) only if they know about the problem.

  4. Use the buffer budgeting approach with senior management. Some PMs tell sponsors: “We have a 15-day buffer built in. I’ll report to you when we’ve consumed 25%, 50%, and 75% of the buffer — each level triggers a different response.” This gives management visibility without micromanagement.

  5. Manage your own time. Project managers who are disorganised cannot manage their team’s schedule. Researcher Shawn Achor (Harvard) found that people who reduce the activation energy for productive habits are far more likely to sustain them — for example, keeping your schedule and tasks visible so the next step requires zero effort to identify.

  6. Don’t just write lower numbers because someone pressured you. An unrealistic schedule does not make the project faster; it destroys credibility when the dates are missed. Use data and reasoning to defend your estimates — or formally document that the deadline was imposed.

9.17.3 Words of Caution on PM Software

Warning⚠️ PM Software Can Be Misused

Project scheduling software (MS Project, Primavera) is a powerful tool — but it is frequently misused:

  • You MUST enter dependencies for the software to calculate dates automatically. If you enter dates manually (manually scheduled tasks), the software cannot recalculate when things change. The whole benefit of CPM is lost.
  • You MUST enter actual data (actual start, actual finish, % complete) to compare planned vs. actual. A schedule that is never updated is just a picture of the original plan — not a management tool.
  • Many users don’t understand the CPM engine. They override the software’s logic and wonder why the dates don’t update. Proper scheduling software training is essential before relying on the output.

Treat the scheduling software as a CPM calculator and change-impact analyser — not just a Gantt drawing tool.

The critical path ran through Lusail Stadium’s seating bowl and roof structure — the most complex and the last to mobilise. Any activity reaching < 5 days of total float triggered an automatic Managing Director-level review within 24 hours.

The Fixed Deadline Strategy: 1. Each stadium committed to completion 6 months before the tournament — allowing full test event cycles 2. Three rounds of test events per stadium: Fan Experience Tests, Operational Readiness Tests, and FIFA Final Certification 3. A parallel maximum acceleration schedule was pre-approved and ready to activate — with pre-agreed resource profiles and contractor incentive payments — if the primary programme was ever threatened

Outcome:
All 8 stadiums were delivered on or before their committed test event dates. The tournament opened on its announced date. Qatar Stadium 974 — a temporary venue built from shipping containers — was delivered as a surprise late addition and met its commissioning deadline.

Key Scheduling Lessons: 1. When the deadline is absolute, schedule contingency must be proportionally large — planning to finish “just in time” is planning to fail 2. External disruptions (COVID, extreme heat) will invalidate sections of any schedule — the organisations that survived maintained pre-developed recovery plans 3. Risk-driven sequencing: the most complex activities (Lusail’s roof) were started earliest and given the most resources 4. Automatic escalation triggers on critical float are more reliable than human judgment — at 5 days of float with 30,000 workers in play, human review is too slow

## Why Time Management Matters [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

Time management is frequently cited as the **most challenging** dimension of the triple constraint. Schedule issues are the **primary source of conflict** on IT and engineering projects — ahead of budget disputes or scope changes.

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ The Schedule Challenge

- **Time is the least flexible constraint**: You can find extra budget; you can negotiate scope — but you cannot recover lost calendar time.
- **Schedule overruns are visible**: Clients see delays immediately; cost overruns can be hidden longer.
- **IT projects are persistently late**: Studies show the average IT project overruns its schedule by 70%; the Standish Group CHAOS Report consistently identifies schedule as the top failure dimension.
:::

### Individual and Cultural Differences

People's relationship with time varies — this affects how teams approach schedules:

| Dimension | Description | Scheduling Implication |
|-----------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Structured (J-type)** — Myers-Briggs | Plans ahead, wants defined milestones, dislikes uncertainty | Follows schedules well; may over-engineer early planning |
| **Flexible (P-type)** — Myers-Briggs | Adapts as they go, resists rigid timelines, works under pressure | Tends to use up float; milestone reminders are essential |
| **Monochronic cultures** | Sequential focus; punctuality is a value; time is a resource | Will adhere to schedules; expect dates to be honoured |
| **Polychronic cultures** | Multiple simultaneous activities; relationships over deadlines | Need clearer milestone expectations and follow-up |

*Awareness of these differences helps PMs design realistic schedules and communicate deadlines effectively.*

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}

### Activity List and Activity Attributes

| Attribute | Description |

| **Activity Name** | Short descriptive label |

| **Assumptions** | What is assumed to be true (affects duration and cost) |

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## 💡 Who Should Estimate?

The people who will **actually do the work** should provide duration estimates — not the project manager alone. They have the most accurate knowledge of the effort involved, the risks, and the constraints. The PM's role is to integrate estimates and challenge unrealistic ones.
:::

### Duration Estimation Methods

::: {.panel-tabset}

## Analogous Estimating
**Use historical data from similar projects**

- Fast and low effort
- Less accurate
- Best for early-stage estimates

*Example:* "Our last 50MW substation took 18 months, so this 60MW substation will take about 21 months"

Accuracy: ±25% to ±50%

## Parametric Estimating
**Use statistical relationships between variables**

- Uses unit rates or formulas
- More accurate than analogous
- Requires reliable data

*Example:* "Excavation rate for this soil type is 120m³/day with a 20-tonne excavator. We have 2,400m³ to excavate, so: 2,400 ÷ 120 = 20 days"

Accuracy: ±10% to ±25%

## Three-Point (PERT) Estimating
**Use optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic estimates**

Three estimates for each activity:
- **Optimistic (O):** Best case, everything goes right
- **Most Likely (M):** Most realistic estimate
- **Pessimistic (P):** Worst case, everything goes wrong

**PERT Expected Duration:**
$$t_E = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6}$$

**PERT Standard Deviation:**
$$\sigma = \frac{P - O}{6}$$

**PERT Variance:**
$$\sigma^2 = \left(\frac{P - O}{6}\right)^2$$

## Bottom-Up Estimating
**Estimate each activity individually, then aggregate**

- Most accurate method
- Most time-consuming
- Best for detailed planning

*Process:*
1. Identify all activities
2. Estimate each individually with subject matter experts
3. Sum all activities for total project duration
4. Add buffer/contingency

Accuracy: ±5% to ±15%

:::

## Effort, Duration, and Elapsed Time

These three terms are often confused but have distinct meanings:

| Term | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Effort** | The total amount of work required (person-hours) | A task requires 40 person-hours of work |
| **Duration** | The calendar time from start to finish | That same task takes 5 working days (8h/day × 5 = 40h) |
| **Elapsed time** | Calendar time including weekends, holidays, waiting | Concrete curing takes 7 elapsed days even if no one works |

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Key Distinctions

- A task that needs **40 hours of effort** by one person at 8h/day = **5 working days of duration**
- The same task with **two people** = still 40 hours of effort, but ~**2.5 days of duration** (with coordination overhead)
- A physical process like **paint drying** adds **elapsed time** (duration) with zero additional effort

*Beware the common mistake of treating effort = duration. A PM who assigns two people to a 40-hour task and shows 2.5 days in the schedule must verify that the task is actually parallelisable — not all work can be divided.*
:::

## Estimating Activity Resources [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

Before finalising duration estimates, the project manager must determine **what resources** are needed for each activity. Resources include:

- **Human resources** — specific people, roles, or skill sets
- **Equipment** — machinery, vehicles, test equipment, lab facilities
- **Materials** — hardware, raw materials, software licences

### Resource Breakdown Structure (RBS)

Just as the WBS breaks down the scope, the **Resource Breakdown Structure (RBS)** categorises all project resources hierarchically. It helps identify potential resource conflicts and ensures every activity has its resource needs documented.

### The Resource-Duration Chicken-and-Egg Problem

Resource estimation and duration estimation are **interdependent** — which makes scheduling an iterative process:

- Duration depends on how many resources are assigned
- Resource availability depends on the schedule

The practical approach: estimate duration assuming a *standard* resource assignment, then level the resources (resolve over-allocations) afterwards.

### Mythical Man-Month — Brooks' Law

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Adding People to a Late Software Project Makes It Later

Frederick Brooks in *"The Mythical Man-Month"* (1975) identified a counter-intuitive truth about software projects:

> "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later."

**Why?** Because:
1. New team members need **ramp-up time** (they consume effort from existing members)
2. **Communication overhead** increases with each additional person: $n(n-1)/2$ communication channels for $n$ people
3. Many software tasks are **sequential, not parallel** — a woman can have one baby in 9 months; nine women cannot have one baby in one month

*Implication: Do not reflexively crash a software project by adding people. Better to focus existing team, reduce scope, or extend the deadline.*
:::

### Resource Leveling

When the initial schedule creates resource **over-allocations** (a person assigned to more work than available hours allow), **resource leveling** resolves conflicts by:

- Delaying non-critical activities within their available float
- Shifting resources from non-critical to critical tasks
- Extending the schedule when float is insufficient

In MS Project: **Resource tab → Level Resources** applies automatic leveling. The schedule end date will typically move later. The PM must then negotiate whether this is acceptable or whether additional resources must be sourced.

### Lag and Lead Time

- **Lag:** A delay after a dependency is satisfied
  - *Example:* Concrete curing — next activity starts 7 days after pouring finishes (FS + 7 days lag)
- **Lead:** An overlap — the successor activity starts before the predecessor finishes
  - *Example:* Begin procurement drawings while detailed design is still 80% complete (FS - 2 weeks lead)

### Types of Dependencies

Beyond the four relationship types (FS/SS/FF/SF), dependencies are also classified by their **source**:

| Type | Also Called | Definition | Example |
|------|-------------|-----------|---------|
| **Mandatory** | Hard logic | Inherent in the nature of the work — cannot be changed | Foundation must cure before walls are built (physics) |
| **Discretionary** | Soft logic, preferred logic | Based on best practice or preference — *can* be changed if needed | PMs prefer to complete testing before documentation, but could overlap |
| **External** | — | Depends on a party outside the project team | Regulatory approval must be received before construction begins |

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Be Careful with Discretionary Dependencies

Discretionary (soft) dependencies are often added out of habit or preference. They reduce scheduling flexibility — and under schedule pressure, teams may break them anyway (creating risk). Review all soft logic before baselineing the schedule. Only keep discretionary dependencies that are genuinely important.
:::

## Network Diagrams — Activity on Arrow (AOA) [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

The **Activity on Arrow (AOA)** method — also called the **Arrow Diagramming Method (ADM)** — places **activities on arrows** and uses **numbered circles (nodes)** to represent events (start/finish milestones):

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 AOA Rules

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Activities** | Represented by arrows (→); label shows the activity name and duration |
| **Events / Nodes** | Numbered circles (①②③…) at the tail (start) and head (finish) of each arrow |
| **Node rule** | An activity can **only start** after **all** activities arriving at its tail node are complete |
| **Dummy Activities** | Dashed arrows (- - ▶) with zero duration; used to show logic that cannot be expressed directly |
| **Dependency type** | **Finish-to-Start (FS) only** — AOA cannot represent SS, FF, or SF lags |
| **Identification** | Each activity is uniquely identified by its *i-j* node pair (e.g., activity 1-2) |
| **Usage today** | Less common than AON; still used in civil engineering, construction, and CPM/PERT exams |
:::

### AOA Dummy Activities

A **dummy activity** is a dashed arrow with **zero duration** and **zero cost**. It represents a **logical dependency only** — it adds no real work.

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Two Reasons You Need a Dummy

**Reason 1 — Uniqueness constraint:** Two real activities share the exact same start node *and* end node. In AOA every activity is identified by its i-j pair. If activity A and activity B both run from node ① to node ②, they are indistinguishable. A dummy gives one of them a unique node.

**Reason 2 — Logic constraint:** You need to express that activity C depends on *both* A and B, while activity D depends *only* on B — not on A. You cannot draw this correctly in AOA without a dummy.
:::

#### Reason 1 — Uniqueness: Two Parallel Activities

**Given activities:**

| Activity | Description | Predecessor(s) | Duration |
|----------|-------------|----------------|----------|
| A | Design UI | — | 3 days |
| B | Write Specs | — | 2 days |
| C | Develop Module | **A and B** | 5 days |

> A and B both start independently and both must finish before C can begin. Because A and B share the same start node AND would share the same end node, they cannot be uniquely identified in AOA — a dummy is required.

**Wrong** (ambiguous — both are "activity 1-2"):

```{mermaid}
%%| fig-cap: "WRONG: A and B both share nodes ①→②; they cannot be uniquely identified"
flowchart LR
    N1(["①"])
    N2(["②"])
    N3(["③"])

    N1 -->|"A — 3d"| N2
    N1 -->|"B — 2d"| N2
    N2 -->|"C — 5d"| N3

    style N1 fill:#b91c1c,color:#fff
    style N2 fill:#b91c1c,color:#fff
```

**Correct** (dummy gives B a unique i-j pair):

```{mermaid}
%%| fig-cap: "CORRECT: dummy ③→② gives activity B the unique pair 1-3; C starts after both A and B are done"
flowchart LR
    N1(["①"])
    N2(["②"])
    N3(["③"])
    N4(["④"])

    N1 -->|"A — 3d"| N2
    N1 -->|"B — 2d"| N3
    N3 -.->|"Dummy (0d)"| N2
    N2 -->|"C — 5d"| N4

    style N1 fill:#1d4ed8,color:#fff
    style N4 fill:#b91c1c,color:#fff
    style N3 fill:#f59e0b,color:#fff
```

*Node ② now receives: A (from ①→②) AND the dummy (from ③→②, carrying B's completion). So C waits for both A and B — correct logic, and every activity has a unique i-j identifier.*

#### Reason 2 — Logic: Split Dependency (Software Release Example)

**Given activities:**

| Activity | Description | Predecessor(s) | Duration |
|----------|-------------|----------------|----------|
| A | Gather Requirements | — | 3 days |
| B | Create UI Mockups | — | 2 days |
| C | Develop Features | **A and B** | 5 days |
| D | Write User Manual | **B only** | 2 days |
| E | System Testing | C | 3 days |

> The critical dependency: C needs both A and B, but D needs B only. Drawing D and C both starting from the same node would force D to also wait for A — a false dependency. A dummy is required to correctly isolate the split.

**Requirement**: C needs A *and* B; D needs B *only*.

**Step-by-step AOA construction:**

1. Since D needs **only B**, B must end at a node where **only B** arrives — that becomes D's start node.
2. Since C needs **both A and B**, C must start from a node where both arrive.  
3. A already ends at its own node. Add a **dummy from B's end-node to A's end-node** to carry B's completion into C's prerequisite check.

```{mermaid}
%%| fig-cap: "AOA — Software Release Project with dummy activity. D starts from ② (only B flows in); C starts from ③ (A plus dummy-from-B flow in)."
flowchart LR
    N1(["① Start"])
    N2(["②<br/>B done"])
    N3(["③<br/>A+B done"])
    N4(["④"])
    N5(["⑤"])
    N6(["⑥ End"])

    N1 -->|"B — 2d"| N2
    N1 -->|"A — 3d"| N3
    N2 -.->|"Dummy (0d)"| N3
    N2 -->|"D — 2d"| N5
    N3 -->|"C — 5d"| N4
    N4 -->|"E — 3d"| N6
    N5 --> N6

    style N1 fill:#1d4ed8,color:#fff
    style N6 fill:#b91c1c,color:#fff
    style N2 fill:#0f766e,color:#fff
    style N3 fill:#7c3aed,color:#fff
    style N2 fill:#0f766e,color:#fff
```

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## ✅ Verify the Logic

| Activity | Starts from | What flows into that node | Correct? |
|----------|-------------|--------------------------|----------|
| **D** | Node ② | **B only** (①→②) | ✅ D needs only B |
| **C** | Node ③ | **A** (①→③) **+ Dummy** (②→③, carrying B) | ✅ C needs A and B |
| **E** | Node ④ | C (③→④) | ✅ E needs only C |

The **dummy arrow ②→③** is the key: it carries B's completion into node ③ without creating a false dependency between A and D.
:::

::: {.callout-important icon="false"}
## ❌ Common Mistake — Wrong Dummy Direction

If you draw the dummy as **②→③** where ② is *A's* end and ③ is *B's* end, then D (starting from ③) would also inherit A — giving D a false dependency on A. Always ensure D's start node has **only its required predecessors** flowing into it.
:::

## Network Diagrams — Activity on Node (AON) [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

The **Activity on Node (AON)** method — also called the **Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM)** — places **activities inside boxes (nodes)** and uses **arrows to show dependencies**:

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 AON Rules

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Activities** | Represented by boxes/rectangles; contain name, ID, and duration |
| **Arrows** | Show dependencies only — the arrow means "must finish before" (for FS) |
| **Dependency types** | **FS, SS, FF, SF** — all four types supported; lags and leads can be shown |
| **Dummy activities** | **Never needed** — every dependency is an explicit arrow |
| **Start / End nodes** | Optional "Start" and "End" milestone nodes used to clearly bound the network |
| **Used by** | All modern PM software: MS Project, Primavera P6, Oracle, etc. |
:::

### AOA vs. AON — Side-by-Side Comparison

| Feature | AOA (Arrow Diagramming) | AON (Precedence Diagramming) |
|---------|------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Activities represented as** | Arrows | Boxes (nodes) |
| **Nodes/circles represent** | Events / milestones | Activities themselves |
| **Dependencies shown by** | Numbered node pairs (i-j) | Explicit arrows between boxes |
| **Dependency types supported** | FS only | FS, SS, FF, SF (with lags) |
| **Dummy activities needed?** | Sometimes (for logic and uniqueness) | **Never** |
| **Reading complexity** | Harder — must trace arrows through nodes | Easier — each box is one activity |
| **Used by PM software?** | Rarely (legacy PERT tools) | Yes — MS Project, Primavera P6 |
| **Best for** | Sequential networks, exams, legacy systems | Any project; modern practice |

> **In practice**, AON is the standard for all modern project scheduling software. AOA is still examined in PMP/CAPM certification exams and appears in older construction scheduling literature.

## Converting Between AOA and AON [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

The same network can always be expressed in both notations. The Software Release example above will be used to demonstrate both directions.

### Converting AOA → AON

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 AOA → AON Conversion Rules

1. **Each real activity arrow in AOA becomes a box (node) in AON.**
2. **Each dummy activity is discarded** — it carries no real work.
3. For every two activities X and Y in AOA where X's end-node is Y's start-node (directly or via a dummy chain), draw an **arrow from X's box to Y's box** in AON.
4. If multiple activities feed into the same start node in AOA, all of them become incoming arrows to the same box in AON.
5. Add a **START** and **END** milestone box to bound the network.
:::

**Step-by-step conversion of the Software Release AOA → AON:**

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **1** | List all real activities: A, B, C, D, E |
| **2** | Identify the dummy: ②→③. Discard it, but **remember what it implied**: node ③ is reached by both A (directly) and B (via dummy from ②). So **B is a predecessor of C** (just like A). |
| **3** | Read predecessors from the AOA node logic: D starts from ② (only B); C starts from ③ (A and B); E starts from ④ (only C) |
| **4** | Draw boxes for A, B, C, D, E and connect with arrows |

### Converting AON → AOA

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 AON → AOA Conversion Rules

1. **Each activity box in AON becomes an arrow in AOA.**
2. **Assign numbered nodes** (circles) at the start and end of each arrow.
3. If two or more activities share the same set of predecessors *and* the same set of successors, they may share start and/or end nodes — **but only if this causes no false dependencies**. If sharing a node would force an unintended dependency, add a **dummy activity**.
4. When activity Y has predecessors X₁ and X₂ in AON: X₁ and X₂ must both end at Y's start node in AOA. If X₁ and X₂ already end at different nodes, add a **dummy** from one of those nodes to the other (or to a new merge node).
5. **Check the uniqueness rule**: no two real activities may share both the same start node and the same end node.
:::

**Step-by-step conversion of the Software Release AON → AOA:**

| Step | What we know from AON | AOA action |
|------|----------------------|-----------|
| **1** | A: no predecessor, successor = C | Arrow A from a new start node ① |
| **2** | B: no predecessor, successors = C and D | Arrow B also from ①. B and A share the same start node (① — fine, different end nodes). |
| **3** | D: predecessor = B only | D can start from B's end node. Let ② = B's end. D: ②→⑤ |
| **4** | C: predecessors = A *and* B | C must start from a node where **both** A and B are complete. A ends at ③. B ends at ②. Add **Dummy ②→③** to carry B's completion into ③. C: ③→④ |
| **5** | E: predecessor = C only | E: ④→⑥ |
| **6** | End: all paths converge at ⑥ | D: ②→⑤→⑥ (add a final merge, or let ⑤ = ⑥ if D and E both end the project) |

        Cx --> Ex["E 3d"]
        Ex --> EN2(["END"])
        Dx --> EN2

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## 💡 Key Insight — Why the Dummy Appears in AOA but Not AON

In AON, you simply draw **two separate arrows** into C (one from A, one from B) — no special mechanism needed.

In AOA, a node is a shared milestone. For C to need *both* A and B, both must arrive at the same node. But D must *not* see A — so D's start node can only have B flowing in. The dummy is the only way to "route" B's completion into the A+B merge node **without forcing D to also wait for A**.

The dummy solves the structural limitation that AOA can only merge dependencies at a single shared node.
:::

## Critical Path Method (CPM) [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

The **Critical Path Method (CPM)** identifies the **longest path through the network** — the sequence of activities that determines the minimum project duration.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 CPM Basic Terms & Formulas

| Term | Formula | Meaning |
|------|---------|---------|
| **ES** — Early Start | $ES = \max(EF \text{ of predecessors})$ | Earliest an activity can start |
| **EF** — Early Finish | $EF = ES + Duration$ | Earliest an activity can finish |
| **LF** — Late Finish | $LF = \min(LS \text{ of successors})$ | Latest it can finish without delaying the project |
| **LS** — Late Start | $LS = LF - Duration$ | Latest it can start without delaying the project |
| **Total Float** | $Float = LS - ES \;=\; LF - EF$ | Flexibility an activity has before it delays the project |
| **Free Float** | $FF = ES_{successor} - EF$ | Flexibility before delaying the *next* activity |
| **Critical Path** | Activities where $Float = 0$ | Longest path — determines total project duration |
| **Critical Activity** | $Float = 0$ | Any delay on this activity delays the entire project |
:::

### Step-by-Step CPM Calculation

**Example Network:**

| Activity | Duration (days) | Predecessors |
|----------|----------------|-------------|
| A | 3 | — |
| B | 5 | A |
| C | 4 | A |
| D | 6 | B |
| E | 3 | B, C |
| F | 4 | D, E |

**Step 1: Forward Pass (Calculate ES and EF)**

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## 💡 Starting Convention
The **first activity always has ES = 0** by convention — the project clock starts at zero. The formula $ES = \max(EF \text{ of predecessors})$ only applies to activities that have predecessors. Similarly, in the backward pass, **LF of the last activity = project duration** (not calculated from successors).
:::

$$ES_A = 0 \quad \text{(by convention — no predecessors)}, \quad EF_A = ES_A + Duration_A = 0 + 3 = 3$$

$$ES_B = EF_A = 3, \quad EF_B = 3 + 5 = 8$$

$$ES_C = EF_A = 3, \quad EF_C = 3 + 4 = 7$$

$$ES_D = EF_B = 8, \quad EF_D = 8 + 6 = 14$$

$$ES_E = \max(EF_B, EF_C) = \max(8, 7) = 8, \quad EF_E = 8 + 3 = 11$$

$$ES_F = \max(EF_D, EF_E) = \max(14, 11) = 14, \quad EF_F = 14 + 4 = 18$$

**Project Duration = 18 days**

**Step 2: Backward Pass (Calculate LS and LF)**

$$LF_F = 18 \quad \text{(by convention — equals project duration, no successors)}, \quad LS_F = LF_F - Duration_F = 18 - 4 = 14$$

$$LF_D = LS_F = 14, \quad LS_D = 14 - 6 = 8$$

$$LF_E = LS_F = 14, \quad LS_E = 14 - 3 = 11$$

$$LF_B = \min(LS_D, LS_E) = \min(8, 11) = 8, \quad LS_B = 8 - 5 = 3$$

$$LF_C = LS_E = 11, \quad LS_C = 11 - 4 = 7$$

$$LF_A = \min(LS_B, LS_C) = \min(3, 7) = 3, \quad LS_A = 3 - 3 = 0$$

**Step 3: Calculate Float**

$$Total\ Float = LS - ES = LF - EF$$

| Activity | ES | EF | LS | LF | Float | Critical? |
|----------|----|----|----|----|-------|-----------|
| A | 0 | 3 | 0 | 3 | **0** | ✅ YES |
| B | 3 | 8 | 3 | 8 | **0** | ✅ YES |
| C | 3 | 7 | 7 | 11 | **4** | NO |
| D | 8 | 14 | 8 | 14 | **0** | ✅ YES |
| E | 8 | 11 | 11 | 14 | **3** | NO |
| F | 14 | 18 | 14 | 18 | **0** | ✅ YES |

**Critical Path: A → B → D → F (18 days)**

### Example 1 — FS Network

**Four activities in a Finish-to-Start network with two parallel branches**

| Activity | Duration | Predecessor | Dependency |
|----------|----------|-------------|------------|
| A | 3 days | — | Start |
| B | 4 days | A | FS |
| C | 2 days | A | FS |

**Forward Pass**

$$ES_B = EF_A = 3,\quad EF_B = 3 + 4 = 7$$

$$ES_C = EF_A = 3,\quad EF_C = 3 + 2 = 5$$

$$ES_D = \max(EF_B,\,EF_C) = \max(7,\,5) = 7,\quad EF_D = 7 + 3 = 10$$

**Project Duration = 10 days**

**Backward Pass**

$$LF_D = 10\;(\text{convention — no successors}),\quad LS_D = 10 - 3 = 7$$

$$LF_B = LS_D = 7,\quad LS_B = 7 - 4 = 3$$

$$LF_C = LS_D = 7,\quad LS_C = 7 - 2 = 5$$

$$LF_A = \min(LS_B,\,LS_C) = \min(3,\,5) = 3,\quad LS_A = 3 - 3 = 0$$

| Activity | ES | EF | LS | LF | Float | Critical? |

**Start-to-Finish (rare): B cannot finish until A starts (+1 day lag)**

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}

The predecessor (A) **starts** before the successor (B) can **finish**.
Classic use case: an old system (B) must keep running until the new system (A) goes live.
$$EF_B \;\geq\; ES_A + \text{lag}$$
In the backward pass: $LS_A \leq LF_B - \text{lag}$

| Activity | Duration | Predecessor | Dependency |
|----------|----------|-------------|------------|
| A | 3 days | — | Start |
| B | 5 days | A | SF+1 |

**Forward Pass**

$$ES_A = 0\;(\text{convention}),\quad EF_A = 0 + 3 = 3$$

B has no start constraint from the SF link (SF only constrains B's *finish*):

$$ES_B = 0\;(\text{no start constraint}),\quad EF_B = 0 + 5 = 5$$

**Project Duration = max(EF_A, EF_B) = max(3, 5) = 5 days**

**Backward Pass**

Neither A nor B has FS successors, so both LFs are set to the project duration:

$$LF_B = 5\;(\text{project end}),\quad LS_B = 5 - 5 = 0$$

SF+1 backward constraint: $LS_A \leq LF_B - \text{lag} = 5 - 1 = 4$

$$LF_A = 5\;(\text{project end}),\quad LS_A = 5 - 3 = 2$$

## PERT Analysis — Handling Schedule Uncertainty [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

**PERT (Programme Evaluation and Review Technique)** extends CPM by acknowledging that activity durations are **uncertain** and modelling them probabilistically.

### PERT Three-Point Estimating

For each activity, obtain three estimates and apply the PERT formulas:

$$t_E = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6} \qquad \sigma = \frac{P - O}{6} \qquad \sigma^2 = \left(\frac{P-O}{6}\right)^2$$

Where:
- **O (Optimistic)** — best-case duration if everything goes perfectly
- **M (Most Likely)** — the most realistic, expected duration under normal conditions
- **P (Pessimistic)** — worst-case duration if most things go wrong
- **$t_E$ (Expected Duration)** — the weighted average; M is weighted 4× because it is the most likely outcome
- **$\sigma$ (Standard Deviation)** — how spread out the estimate is; large σ means high uncertainty
- **$\sigma^2$ (Variance)** — used when combining uncertainty across multiple activities on the critical path (variances add, standard deviations do not)

**Worked calculations:**

| Activity | O | M | P | $O + 4M + P$ | $t_E = \div 6$ | $P - O$ | $\sigma = \div 6$ | $\sigma^2$ |
|----------|---|---|---|-------------|---------------|---------|-------------------|----------|
| Foundation Design | 8 | 12 | 22 | $8+48+22=78$ | **13.00** | $22-8=14$ | **2.33** | **5.44** |
| Structural Design | 12 | 18 | 30 | $12+72+30=114$ | **19.00** | $30-12=18$ | **3.00** | **9.00** |
| Drawing Production | 6 | 9 | 15 | $6+36+15=57$ | **9.50** | $15-6=9$ | **1.50** | **2.25** |
| Approvals | 14 | 21 | 56 | $14+84+56=154$ | **25.67** | $56-14=42$ | **7.00** | **49.00** |

: PERT three-point estimates with full calculation steps {.striped .hover}

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## 💡 Reading the Table

- **Large gap between O and P** (e.g., Approvals: 14 vs 56) → high uncertainty → large σ (7.00) → this activity dominates the project's schedule risk
- **Small gap** (e.g., Drawing Production: 6 vs 15) → lower uncertainty → σ = 1.50 → more predictable
- **$t_E$ is always pulled toward P** when P is far from M — the pessimistic tail has real weight
- **Add variances** (not σ) when computing the overall project standard deviation: $\sigma_{project} = \sqrt{\sum \sigma^2}$
:::

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 💡 ملاحظة

نشاط **"الموافقات"** له أكبر فجوة بين O و P (14 مقابل 56)، مما يعني **عدم يقين عالٍ**. هذا النشاط هو المصدر الرئيسي لمخاطر الجدول الزمني.
:::

**Project Expected Duration** — sum all $t_E$ values on the critical path:

$$T_E = 13.00 + 19.00 + 9.50 + 25.67 = \mathbf{67.17 \text{ days}}$$

**Project Standard Deviation** — sum the **variances** (not σ), then take the square root:

$$\sigma_{project} = \sqrt{\sigma^2_1 + \sigma^2_2 + \sigma^2_3 + \sigma^2_4} = \sqrt{5.44 + 9.00 + 2.25 + 49.00} = \sqrt{65.69} = \mathbf{8.11 \text{ days}}$$

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Why Add Variances, Not Standard Deviations?

You **cannot** add σ values directly: $2.33 + 3.00 + 1.50 + 7.00 = 13.83$ — this overstates uncertainty.

Variances add correctly because they measure independent squared spread. You take the square root **after** summing to get back to days:
$$\sigma_{project} = \sqrt{\sum \sigma^2} <br/>eq \sum \sigma$$
:::

### Probability of Completion by Target Date

Using the normal distribution:

$$Z = \frac{T_{target} - T_{expected}}{\sigma_{project}}$$

Where $T_{target}$ is the desired completion date and $T_{expected}$ is the calculated expected completion.

The Z-score tells you **how many standard deviations** the target date is above the expected duration. You then look up that Z-score in the **Standard Normal Distribution Table** below to find the probability:

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Standard Normal Distribution (Z-Table) — Selected Values

| Z | Probability P(Z ≤ z) | Meaning |
|------|----------------------|---------|
| 0.00 | 50% | Target = expected duration — 50/50 chance |
| 0.50 | 69% | Half a standard deviation above expected |
| 0.84 | 80% | |
| 1.00 | 84% | One standard deviation above expected |
| 1.28 | 90% | |
| 1.45 | 93% | |
| 1.58 | **94%** | ← used in our example below |
| 1.645 | **95%** | ← common project management target |
| 2.00 | 97.7% | Two standard deviations above expected |
| 2.326 | **99%** | |
| 2.576 | 99.5% | |
| 3.00 | 99.9% | |

: Z-table reference for PERT probability calculations {.striped .hover}

**How to read:** A Z-score of 1.645 means the target date is 1.645 standard deviations above the expected finish — and there is a 95% probability the project finishes by that date.
:::

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Example PERT Probability Calculation

Using the values calculated above from our four activities:

**Critical path expected duration:** $T_E = 67.17$ days  
**Project standard deviation:** $\sigma_{project} = 8.11$ days  
**Client deadline:** 80 days  

$$Z = \frac{80 - 67.17}{8.11} = \frac{12.83}{8.11} = 1.58$$

From Z-table: P(Z ≤ 1.58) ≈ **94%**

There is a **94% probability** of completing the project within 80 days.

To achieve 95% probability: Need Z = 1.645 → $T = 67.17 + (1.645 \times 8.11) = 67.17 + 13.34 \approx \mathbf{80.5 \text{ days}}$

To achieve 99% probability: Need Z = 2.326 → $T = 67.17 + (2.326 \times 8.11) \approx \mathbf{86.1 \text{ days}}$
:::

### Cost-Slope for Crashing

The **cost-slope** measures how much it costs to shorten an activity by **one unit of time** (one day). It lets you compare activities and always crash the cheapest one first.

$$\text{Cost Slope} = \frac{C_c - C_n}{T_n - T_c}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $C_n$ | Normal Cost — cost under the original schedule |
| $C_c$ | Crash Cost — cost when the activity is accelerated |
| $T_n$ | Normal Duration — original planned duration |
| $T_c$ | Crash Duration — shortest achievable duration |

**Worked calculations for each activity:**

| Activity | $T_n$ | $T_c$ | $C_n$ | $C_c$ | $C_c - C_n$ | $T_n - T_c$ | Cost Slope | Priority |
|----------|-------|-------|-------|-------|------------|------------|------------|----------|
| A | 10d | 7d | $10,000 | $16,000 | $6,000 | 3 days | **$2,000/day** | 🥇 1st |
| B | 8d | 6d | $8,000 | $12,000 | $4,000 | 2 days | **$2,000/day** | 🥇 1st |
| C | 15d | 11d | $20,000 | $32,000 | $12,000 | 4 days | **$3,000/day** | 🥈 2nd |
| D | 12d | 9d | $15,000 | $24,000 | $9,000 | 3 days | **$3,000/day** | 🥈 2nd |

: Cost-slope calculations with priority ranking {.striped .hover}

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## 💡 The Golden Rule of Crashing

**Always crash the critical path activity with the lowest cost-slope first, then re-analyse.**

Only critical path activities matter — crashing a non-critical activity saves zero project time.

| Goal | Best choice |
|------|------------|
| Save cost | Crash A or B ($2,000/day) |
| Save maximum time | Crash C (4 days available) |
| Balanced — save 2 days cheapest | Crash A or B: 2 × $2,000 = **$4,000** vs C or D: 2 × $3,000 = $6,000 |
:::

**Step-by-step crashing process:**

1. Identify activities on the critical path only
2. Calculate cost-slope for each critical activity
3. Sort ascending by cost-slope (cheapest first)
4. Crash the cheapest activity — but do not exceed its maximum crash limit ($T_n - T_c$)
5. **Re-analyse the critical path** — it may shift after crashing
6. Repeat until the target duration is reached

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Crashing Cautions

| Warning | Reason |
|---------|--------|
| Never crash non-critical activities | They don't affect project end date |
| Always re-check the critical path after each crash | A new critical path may emerge |
| Respect each activity's crash limit | Every activity has a minimum achievable duration |
| Watch for quality impact | Excessive acceleration increases error rates |
| Get budget approval first | Crash cost must be formally authorised |
:::

**Quick example — save 3 days at minimum cost:**

Activity A has a crash limit of 3 days (10 → 7) at $2,000/day:

$$\text{Additional cost} = 3 \times \$2{,}000 = \mathbf{\$6{,}000}$$

Alternatively, use Activity B (limit: 2 days) + Activity C (1 day):

$$2 \times \$2{,}000 + 1 \times \$3{,}000 = \$4{,}000 + \$3{,}000 = \mathbf{\$7{,}000}$$

✅ **Optimal: Crash Activity A by 3 days for $6,000**



## Critical Chain Scheduling [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

**Critical Chain Scheduling** is an alternative to CPM developed by Eliyahu Goldratt based on his **Theory of Constraints** (from the novel *"The Goal"*, 1984). It focuses on managing **buffers** rather than individual activity floats.

### The Problem with Traditional Scheduling

When PMs ask team members to estimate task durations, team members build in **individual safety buffers** — often doubling the realistic estimate. This leads to two dysfunctions:

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Parkinson's Law + Murphy's Law = Schedule Disaster

- **Parkinson's Law**: *"Work expands to fill the time available."* If an activity has 10 days in the schedule, it will take 10 days — even if it could be done in 6. People do not finish early; they use the buffer for other work or polishing.
- **Murphy's Law**: *"Anything that can go wrong, will go wrong."* Problems arise anyway, consuming the individual buffers — and the project still runs late.

The result: **individual padding doesn't protect the project end date**, but it does inflate the schedule and slow down the team.
:::

### The Multitasking Problem

Critical chain scheduling addresses the harm caused by **excessive multitasking**. Consider a team member assigned to three parallel tasks (A, B, C), each requiring 10 working days:

| Approach | Task A finishes | Task B finishes | Task C finishes |
|----------|----------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Serial** (one at a time) | Day 10 | Day 20 | Day 30 |
| **Parallel multitasking** (one third each day) | Day 30 | Day 30 | Day 30 |

Parallel multitasking delays every task and adds context-switching overhead. The solution: **focus** — finish one task, then start the next.

### How Critical Chain Scheduling Works

```{mermaid}
%%| fig-cap: "Critical chain scheduling: buffers are consolidated, not distributed"
%%{init: {'flowchart': {'nodeSpacing': 20, 'rankSpacing': 45}}}%%

    A["Task A<br/>(realistic)"] --> B["Task B<br/>(realistic)"] --> C["Task C<br/>(realistic)"] --> PB["PROJECT<br/>BUFFER<br/>🛡️"]
    
    F1["Feeding<br/>Chain 1"] --> FB1["Feeding<br/>Buffer 🛡️"] --> B
    F2["Feeding<br/>Chain 2"] --> FB2["Feeding<br/>Buffer 🛡️"] --> C

    style FB1 fill:#b45309,color:#fff

    style A fill:#1d4ed8,color:#fff

    style C fill:#1d4ed8,color:#fff
```

**Steps in critical chain scheduling:**

1. **Remove individual safety buffers** from activity estimates — use the 50% likely duration (not the padded estimate)
2. **Identify the critical chain** — the longest chain of dependent activities *considering resource constraints* (not just logic links)
3. **Add a Project Buffer** at the end of the critical chain before the project due date — this is where the removed padding goes
4. **Add Feeding Buffers** where non-critical chains feed into the critical chain — to protect the critical chain from delays on feeder paths
5. **Manage the buffer** — track what percentage of the buffer has been consumed vs. how much of the project is complete

### Three Organisational Approaches to Buffers

| Organisation Type | Behaviour | Outcome |
|-------------------|-----------|---------|
| **Hard-nose** | No buffers allowed; pressures people to commit to minimum durations | Team members hide buffers in their own work; no visibility |
| **Lenient** | Allows individual buffers in each task | Parkinson's Law applies; project still runs late |
| **Critical chain** | Removes individual buffers; PM controls an aggregate project buffer | Buffer is visible; PM can see when project is truly at risk |

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## 💡 Why Not Show the Buffer to the Team?

The project buffer should be managed by the **PM**, not consumed by the team. If team members know there is a 15-day buffer, they will use it. The buffer is a management reserve — not extra time for individual tasks.
:::

## Agile Time Management [➕ Extended]{.badge-ext}

Traditional CPM scheduling assumes all work is **defined upfront** — you know every activity before the project starts. Agile projects take a fundamentally different approach.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Agile Scheduling Principles

- **Don't define all work before scheduling**: Only plan in detail what will be done in the next sprint (1–4 weeks)
- **Product Backlog**: A prioritised list of all desired features and work — managed by the **Product Owner**
- **Sprint Backlog**: The subset of backlog items the team commits to completing in the current sprint
- **Velocity**: The team's average output per sprint (in story points or hours), used to predict future delivery
- **Responding to change over following a plan**: The schedule adapts sprint-by-sprint based on what has been learned and what stakeholders now want
:::

### Agile vs. Traditional Scheduling

| Dimension | Traditional (CPM) | Agile |
|-----------|------------------|-------|
| Plan horizon | Full project upfront | Sprint-by-sprint (rolling wave) |
| Schedule document | Gantt chart + network diagram | Sprint board (Kanban/Scrum) |
| Change handling | Formal change control | Expected; backlog is re-prioritised |
| Progress measure | % of activities complete | Working product per sprint |
| Schedule owner | Project Manager | Team + Product Owner together |

*For engineering and construction projects, full CPM scheduling remains essential. For IT software development, agile or hybrid approaches are increasingly standard.*

## Controlling the Schedule [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

Once the project is underway, the schedule must be **monitored and controlled**. The goals of schedule control are:

1. **Know the current status** of the schedule — what is on time, what is behind
2. **Identify the factors** causing schedule changes before they become delays
3. **Determine whether the schedule has changed** and by how much
4. **Manage the actual changes** when they occur

### Tools and Techniques for Schedule Control

| Tool | How It Helps |
|------|-------------|
| **Progress reports** | Team members report actual start, finish, and % complete for their activities |
| **Schedule change control system** | Defines the process for requesting, reviewing, and approving changes to the baseline |
| **Tracking Gantt chart** | Compares planned (grey bars) vs. actual (solid bars) progress visually |
| **Variance analysis** | Calculates schedule variance = $SV = EV - PV$ (see Chapter 7 — Earned Value) |
| **Float/slack monitoring** | Activities with decreasing float are at risk; zero float means the critical path has shifted |
| **PM software** (MS Project, Primavera) | Recalculates CPM automatically when actuals are entered |
| **Resource leveling** | Resolves over-allocations that cause delays |

### Reality Checks and Best Practices

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## 💡 Schedule Control — Reality Checks

1. **Do not plan everyone at 100% capacity.** Real work includes email, meetings, administrative tasks, and unexpected interruptions. Planning at 80–85% capacity is more realistic.

2. **Hold regular progress meetings with the team and stakeholders.** Not to report blame, but to surface problems early when there is still time to respond.

3. **Be honest with your sponsor when the schedule is slipping.** Hiding a delay is worse than surfacing it early — sponsors can help (more resources, scope reduction, deadline extension) only if they know about the problem.

4. **Use the buffer budgeting approach with senior management.** Some PMs tell sponsors: *"We have a 15-day buffer built in. I'll report to you when we've consumed 25%, 50%, and 75% of the buffer — each level triggers a different response."* This gives management visibility without micromanagement.

5. **Manage your own time.** Project managers who are disorganised cannot manage their team's schedule. Researcher Shawn Achor (Harvard) found that people who reduce the *activation energy* for productive habits are far more likely to sustain them — for example, keeping your schedule and tasks visible so the next step requires zero effort to identify.

6. **Don't just write lower numbers because someone pressured you.** An unrealistic schedule does not make the project faster; it destroys credibility when the dates are missed. Use data and reasoning to defend your estimates — or formally document that the deadline was imposed.
:::

### Words of Caution on PM Software

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ PM Software Can Be Misused

Project scheduling software (MS Project, Primavera) is a powerful tool — but it is frequently misused:

- **You MUST enter dependencies** for the software to calculate dates automatically. If you enter dates manually (manually scheduled tasks), the software cannot recalculate when things change. The whole benefit of CPM is lost.
- **You MUST enter actual data** (actual start, actual finish, % complete) to compare planned vs. actual. A schedule that is never updated is just a picture of the original plan — not a management tool.
- **Many users don't understand the CPM engine.** They override the software's logic and wonder why the dates don't update. Proper scheduling software training is essential before relying on the output.

*Treat the scheduling software as a CPM calculator and change-impact analyser — not just a Gantt drawing tool.*
:::

## Case Study — Qatar FIFA World Cup 2022: Scheduling the Unschedulable {.unnumbered}

::: {.callout-important icon="false"}
## 🏭 Case Study: Qatar 2022 — When the Deadline Cannot Move

**Background:**  
On 21 November 2022, the FIFA World Cup opened at Al-Bayt Stadium in Al-Khor, Qatar — the first World Cup hosted by an Arab nation. For Qatar's Supreme Committee for Delivery & Legacy (SC), the scheduling challenge was unique: the tournament date was set in December 2010 and could not shift by even a single day. This created a project scheduling environment where float on the critical path was, ultimately, zero.

**The Scale:**
- **8 world-class stadiums** built or substantially reconstructed (most World Cup hosts refurbish existing facilities)
- **Hard deadline:** 21 November 2022 — fixed 12 years in advance
- **Construction workforce:** 30,000+ workers across stadium sites simultaneously
- **Largest stadium:** Lusail Stadium (88,966 capacity) — the centrepiece venue for the final

**Scheduling Challenges:**

| Challenge | Response |
|-----------|----------|
| Summer temperatures reaching 50°C made daytime work impossible June–September | Shifted all critical construction to **mandatory night shifts** (22:00–06:00); stadium cooling systems tested 18 months early to enable summer commissioning |
| COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020) halted construction for 4–6 weeks | Critical path reassessed immediately; non-critical works suspended; critical path workers resumed in isolated bubbles within 3 weeks |
| Lusail Stadium — largest design, started last | Assigned highest resource density of any stadium; 24-hour operations from 2018; Lusail became first stadium to complete trial events in 2021 despite being the last to start |
| Al-Thumama Stadium required partial structural redesign mid-construction | Design frozen at 60% completion; structural changes fast-tracked through review cycles; construction sequence reordered around the redesigned elements |
| All 8 stadiums required fully operational cooling systems *before* commissioning began | MEP commissioning placed on the critical path from contract start — 3 months bought back by accelerating installation sequence |

**CPM Application:**  
The SC's scheduling team used **Oracle Primavera P6 Enterprise** with a master programme of **35,000+ activities**. The critical path ran through Lusail Stadium's seating bowl and roof structure — the most complex and the last to mobilise. Any activity reaching **< 5 days of total float** triggered an automatic Managing Director-level review within 24 hours.

**The Fixed Deadline Strategy:**
1. Each stadium committed to completion **6 months before the tournament** — allowing full test event cycles
2. Three rounds of test events per stadium: Fan Experience Tests, Operational Readiness Tests, and FIFA Final Certification
3. A parallel **maximum acceleration schedule** was pre-approved and ready to activate — with pre-agreed resource profiles and contractor incentive payments — if the primary programme was ever threatened

**Outcome:**  
All 8 stadiums were delivered on or before their committed test event dates. The tournament opened on its announced date. Qatar Stadium 974 — a temporary venue built from shipping containers — was delivered as a surprise late addition and met its commissioning deadline.

**Key Scheduling Lessons:**
1. When the deadline is absolute, schedule contingency must be proportionally large — planning to finish "just in time" is planning to fail
2. External disruptions (COVID, extreme heat) will invalidate sections of any schedule — the organisations that survived maintained pre-developed recovery plans
3. Risk-driven sequencing: the most complex activities (Lusail's roof) were started earliest and given the most resources
4. Automatic escalation triggers on critical float are more reliable than human judgment — at 5 days of float with 30,000 workers in play, human review is too slow

*Sources: @fifaqatar2022; @sc2023legacy*
:::
