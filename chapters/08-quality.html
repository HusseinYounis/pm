14  Quality Management

Chapter 8 · Execution & Control

Quality Management

Planning, assuring, and controlling quality to deliver engineering projects that meet specifications and client expectations.

Learning Outcomes

Tip🎯 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish between quality planning, quality assurance, and quality control
  2. Develop a Quality Management Plan for an engineering project
  3. Apply all Seven Basic Tools of Quality: control charts, Pareto analysis, fishbone diagrams, checklists, scatter diagrams, histograms, and run charts
  4. Calculate statistical sample sizes and explain when to use sampling
  5. Explain the Six Sigma DMAIC methodology and belt system
  6. Describe continuous improvement philosophies: Kaizen, Lean, and Kanban
  7. Identify the types of software tests and explain when each is applied
  8. Summarise the contributions of Deming, Juran, Crosby, and Ishikawa
  9. Explain ISO 9001 and its role in project quality management
  10. Analyse the cost of quality (prevention vs. appraisal vs. failure costs)

14.1 What is Quality in Engineering Projects? 📋 Required

Note📖 Two Dimensions of Quality

Quality of Design (Fitness for Purpose):
Does the delivered product meet the intended purpose? Did we deliver what the client NEEDED?

Quality of Conformance (Conformance to Specifications):
Was the product built exactly to the approved specifications, drawings, and standards?

Example: A bridge designed for 40-tonne trucks but only used by cars is HIGH quality of conformance (built as designed) but potentially POOR quality of design (over-engineered = waste). A bridge designed for 100-tonne loads but built with B-grade steel is HIGH quality of design but POOR quality of conformance.

14.1.1 Grade vs. Quality

High Grade Low Grade
High Quality Premium product built to spec Budget product built to spec
Low Quality Premium product with defects Budget product with defects

Grade is the level of performance requirements; Quality is whether those requirements are met.

14.2 The Quality Management Process 📋 Required

Note📖 QA vs. QC — The Critical Distinction

Quality Assurance (QA): Focuses on processes — “Are we doing the right things the right way?” - Proactive and process-oriented - Performed DURING the project - Examples: Process audits, design reviews, peer checks, construction supervision

Quality Control (QC): Focuses on outputs — “Does the product meet requirements?” - Reactive and product-oriented - Applied to completed work - Examples: Material testing, dimensional surveys, pressure testing, functional testing

14.4 The Quality Management Plan 📋 Required

A Quality Management Plan (QMP) defines how quality will be managed throughout the project:

  • QMP Contents
  • Inspection and Test Plan (ITP)
  • Non-Conformance Management
  1. Quality objectives — measurable quality targets linked to project scope
  2. Quality standards — applicable codes, standards, specifications (e.g., AS/NZS, ISO, ASTM)
  3. Quality responsibilities — who is accountable for quality activities
  4. Inspection and Test Plan (ITP) — what will be tested, by whom, and when
  5. Non-conformance procedure — how defects are identified, documented, and resolved
  6. Document control — version control and distribution of quality documents
  7. Audit schedule — when QA audits will be conducted

The ITP is the core QC tool — it specifies every inspection and test:

Concrete 28-day cylinder test AS 1012.9 1 set per 50m³ NATA Lab Review (R) ≥ N32 (32MPa)

Steel weld NDT (ultrasonic) AS 2205 10% random Certified UT tech Review (R) Class B
Pipeline hydrostatic test Pressure test AS 2885 Each segment Engineer Witness (W) 1.5× operating pressure, 1 hour

14.5.5 5. Scatter Diagram

A scatter diagram (scatter plot) helps determine whether a relationship (correlation) exists between two variables. The closer data points cluster around a diagonal line, the stronger the correlation.

Note📖 Reading a Scatter Diagram
Pattern Interpretation
Points cluster along rising diagonal Positive correlation — as X increases, Y increases
Points cluster along falling diagonal Negative correlation — as X increases, Y decreases
Points scattered randomly No correlation — the variables are independent

Example: Plot concrete slump (X) vs. compressive strength (Y). A negative correlation would suggest higher water/cement ratio reduces strength.

Strong negative correlation: higher w/c ratio → lower strength. Use this to set and enforce maximum w/c ratio in your specification.

14.5.6 6. Histogram

A histogram is a bar chart showing the frequency distribution of a quality variable — how many occurrences fall within each interval. It reveals the shape of a distribution: symmetric, skewed, or bimodal.

The distribution is roughly bell-shaped, centred around 36–39 MPa. The one result below 30 MPa is an outlier requiring investigation.

Warning⚠️ Histogram vs. Bar Chart

A histogram shows the distribution of a continuous variable (like strength or temperature) grouped into ranges. A bar chart compares distinct categories. The bars in a histogram touch each other; bar chart bars have gaps.

14.5.7 7. Run Chart

A run chart (also used for stratification) displays the history and pattern of a process variable over time — without control limits. Unlike control charts, run charts show trends and cycles that indicate non-random patterns.

Note📖 What to Look for in a Run Chart
  • Trend — 7+ consecutive points consistently rising or falling → process is drifting
  • Run — 8+ consecutive points on the same side of the median → process is not centred
  • Cycle — repeating up-down pattern → likely shift-based or equipment-based variation

Weeks 9–16 show a clear upward trend (8 consecutive increasing points). This signals a deteriorating process — investigate and act before the trend worsens.

14.5.8 Statistical Sampling

Statistical sampling involves selecting a representative subset of items from a population for inspection — instead of testing every single item (100% inspection).

Note📖 Sample Size Formula

\[n = 0.25 \times \left(\frac{CF}{e}\right)^2\]

Where: - \(n\) = required sample size - \(CF\) = certainty factor (from standard table) - \(e\) = acceptable error rate (as a decimal)

Desired Certainty Certainty Factor (CF)
90% 1.65
95% 1.96
98% 2.33
99% 2.58
Tip💡 Solved Example

You want to be 95% certain that your sample result is within ±5% of the true population value.

\[n = 0.25 \times \left(\frac{1.96}{0.05}\right)^2 = 0.25 \times (39.2)^2 = 0.25 \times 1536.64 \approx \mathbf{384}\]

You need a sample of 384 items.

If you can accept more error (±10%), the required sample size drops to 96. There is always a trade-off between precision and inspection cost.

When to use statistical sampling: - Concrete cube testing (test a sample of pours, not every cubic metre) - Software testing (test a representative sample of code paths) - Incoming material inspection (test a sample from each batch) - Customer satisfaction surveys (survey a sample of users)

14.6 Performing Quality Assurance — Kaizen, Lean, and Kanban 📋 Required

Quality Assurance (QA) includes all activities related to satisfying quality standards. Beyond audits and process checks, modern QA embraces continuous improvement philosophies:

14.6.1 Kaizen (改善)

Kaizen is the Japanese philosophy of continuous incremental improvement — every employee, at every level, contributes small improvements every day.

Note📖 Kaizen Principles
  1. Go to Gemba — observe the actual work being done; improvements come from the field, not the office
  2. Eliminate waste (Muda) — identify and remove activities that add no value
  3. Standardise then improve — you cannot improve what is not standardised
  4. Small steps, not big leaps — many small improvements are more sustainable than occasional breakthroughs
  5. Everyone participates — quality improvement is not just the QA department’s job

Example in engineering: A site team notices that waiting for RFI responses causes an average 2-day delay per week. Kaizen event: team designs a 24-hour RFI response target with escalation — rework and delays drop by 60%.

14.6.2 Lean

Lean focuses on maximising customer value while minimising waste. Lean evaluates every activity as either value-adding or waste (non-value-adding):

Lean Waste Category Description Engineering Example
Overproduction Producing more than needed Drawing revisions not yet needed
Waiting Idle time while waiting for inputs Waiting for material delivery, approval
Transport Unnecessary movement of materials Moving equipment across site unnecessarily
Over-processing More work than required Preparing 100-page reports when 2 pages needed
Inventory Excess materials or work in progress Too much concrete on site; unprocessed RFIs
Motion Unnecessary movement of people Walking to a distant printer 20×/day
Defects Rework and rectification Concrete pour requiring full demolition

Lean PM projects that eliminate waste achieve 20–40% reductions in project duration and cost.

14.6.3 Kanban

Kanban is a visual workflow management system that uses a Kanban board to limit work-in-progress (WIP) and improve flow:

Note📖 Kanban Core Properties
  1. Visualise the workflow — all tasks visible on a board (To Do / In Progress / Review / Done)
  2. Limit WIP (Work in Progress) — each column has a maximum number of items; prevents overloading
  3. Manage flow — measure how long tasks take to move through the system (cycle time)
  4. Make process policies explicit — everyone agrees on what “Done” means for each column
  5. Identify improvement opportunities — bottlenecks become visible when WIP limits are hit

Kanban originated in Toyota manufacturing (1940s) and is now widely used in software development, design teams, and construction project offices.

How Kanban differs from Scrum: Kanban is flow-based (continuous delivery); Scrum is iteration-based (fixed-length sprints). Kanban does not have defined roles or ceremonies — it is applied to the existing workflow.

14.7 Six Sigma 📋 Required

Six Sigma is a comprehensive, data-driven quality methodology that aims to reduce defects to no more than 3.4 per million opportunities — achieving near-perfect quality.

Note📖 Six Sigma — Basic Facts
Item Detail
Goal ≤ 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO)
Statistical basis A process at 6σ means the nearest specification limit is 6 standard deviations from the mean
Origin Motorola (1986); popularised by General Electric (Jack Welch, 1990s)
Driven by Customer needs, facts and data, statistical analysis
Applies to Manufacturing, IT, healthcare, construction, services

14.7.3 Six Sigma and Statistics

Note📖 Standard Deviation and Sigma Levels

The term sigma (σ) is the standard deviation — a measure of process variability. The sigma level indicates how many standard deviations fit between the process mean and the nearest specification limit:

Sigma Level DPMO Quality (%)
1σ 691,462 30.9%
2σ 308,538 69.1%
3σ 66,807 93.3%
4σ 6,210 99.4%
5σ 233 99.98%
6σ 3.4 99.9997%

Most industries operate at 3σ–4σ. Airlines achieve ~6σ for fatal accident rates; hospital medication error rates are often at 2σ–3σ.

\[\text{At 6σ: } P(\text{defect}) = 3.4 \times 10^{-6}\]

14.7.4 Six Sigma and Project Management

Six Sigma projects are managed like projects: - Business case and project charter — defines scope, goals, and team - Schedule and budget — tracked using PM tools - Team structure — Black Belt as project manager; Champion as sponsor - Stage-gate reviews — approval at each DMAIC phase before proceeding - Benefits realisation — financial savings tracked and reported after Control phase

14.8 Software Testing 📋 Required

Testing is a critical QC activity for IT and software projects. Testing alone cannot guarantee quality — it must be part of a broader QA strategy — but it is an essential defect-detection mechanism.

14.8.1 Types of Tests

  • Unit Testing
  • Integration Testing
  • System Testing
  • User Acceptance Testing (UAT)
  • Regression Testing
  • Performance Testing

Tests individual functions or modules in isolation

  • Performed by developers during coding
  • Uses test frameworks (JUnit, pytest, NUnit)
  • Verifies: Does this function do what it is supposed to do?
  • Finds bugs early, when they are cheapest to fix
  • Target: 80%+ code coverage for critical systems

Tests how modules/components work together

  • Performed after unit testing
  • Finds interface defects (data passed incorrectly between modules)
  • Approaches: Top-down, bottom-up, big-bang
  • Verifies: Do the connected parts communicate correctly?

Tests the complete, integrated system against requirements

  • Black-box testing — tests behaviour, not internal code
  • Covers: functional requirements, performance, security, usability
  • Performed in an environment that mirrors production
  • The primary stage for finding defects before user acceptance

End users test the system against real business scenarios

  • Final stage before go-live
  • Users confirm the system meets their needs
  • Not about finding bugs — about confirming fitness for purpose
  • A failed UAT does not always mean the system is broken; sometimes requirements were wrong

Re-runs existing tests after changes to verify nothing was broken

  • Run after every bug fix or new feature
  • Automated regression suites are essential for large systems
  • Prevents the “fix one thing, break two others” problem

Tests system behaviour under load

  • Load testing: normal expected load
  • Stress testing: beyond expected load (find breaking point)
  • Spike testing: sudden traffic increases
  • Endurance testing: sustained load over long period
Warning⚠️ Testing Alone Is Not Enough

“Testing shows the presence of bugs, not their absence.” — Edsger Dijkstra

Testing finds defects after they exist. The most cost-effective strategy is to prevent defects through: - Clear, unambiguous requirements - Design reviews and peer inspections - Coding standards and code reviews - Continuous integration (automated testing on every commit)

The 1-10-100 rule applies: A requirement defect costs $1 to fix in design; $10 in development; $100 in production.

14.9 Quality Experts 📋 Required

Several pioneers shaped modern quality management thinking. Every project manager should know their key contributions:

  • W. Edwards Deming
  • Joseph M. Juran
  • Philip Crosby
  • Kaoru Ishikawa
  • Summary Comparison

W. Edwards Deming (1900–1993) — American statistician and management consultant; widely credited with Japan’s quality revolution post-WWII.

Key contributions: - PDCA Cycle (Plan-Do-Check-Act) — the foundation of continuous improvement - 14 Points for Management — a framework for transforming organisational quality culture - System of Profound Knowledge — understanding systems, variation, psychology, and theory of knowledge - Emphasis on management’s role: Deming believed 85% of quality problems are management problems, not worker problems

Famous quote: “In God we trust; all others bring data.”

Joseph M. Juran (1904–2008) — Romanian-born American engineer and quality expert.

Key contributions: - Quality Trilogy: Quality Planning → Quality Control → Quality Improvement - Pareto Principle applied to quality: 80% of problems from 20% of causes (the “vital few”) - Fitness for use: Quality is not just conformance to spec — the product must be fit for its intended purpose - “All improvement takes place project by project” — quality improvement requires project discipline

Juran’s Quality Handbook is considered the bible of quality management.

Philip Crosby (1926–2001) — American businessman and quality consultant.

Key contributions: - Zero Defects concept — the performance standard should be doing things right the first time, every time - Quality is Free (1979 book) — argued that the cost of prevention is always less than the cost of failures - Four Absolutes of Quality Management: 1. Quality is conformance to requirements (not goodness) 2. The system of quality is prevention (not appraisal) 3. The performance standard is Zero Defects (not AQL) 4. The measurement of quality is the Price of Non-conformance (not indices) - 14 Steps to Quality Improvement — a practical implementation programme

Kaoru Ishikawa (1915–1989) — Japanese chemical engineer; pioneer of quality circles.

Key contributions: - Fishbone (Ishikawa) Diagram — cause-and-effect diagram for root cause analysis - Quality Circles — small groups of workers who meet regularly to identify and solve quality problems in their own work - Company-wide quality control (CWQC) — quality is everyone’s responsibility, not just the QA department - Seven Basic Tools of Quality: Cause-and-effect diagram, checksheet, control chart, histogram, Pareto chart, scatter diagram, flowchart/run chart

Ishikawa popularised the idea that front-line workers, given the right tools and training, can solve most quality problems themselves.

Expert Core Idea Key Tool/Concept
Deming Reduce variation; management causes most quality problems PDCA Cycle, 14 Points
Juran Quality improvement is project-by-project; fitness for use Quality Trilogy, Pareto for quality
Crosby Zero Defects; quality is free; prevention beats inspection Four Absolutes, QI 14 Steps
Ishikawa Everyone owns quality; front-line workers solve problems Fishbone diagram, Quality Circles

14.11 Cost of Quality 📋 Required

The Cost of Quality (CoQ) model recognises that ALL quality activities have a cost — and that investing in prevention is far cheaper than paying for failures:

  • Prevention Costs
  • Appraisal Costs
  • Internal Failure Costs
  • External Failure Costs

Costs to prevent defects (proactive): - Quality planning and QMP development - Staff training and competency assessment - Design reviews and peer checks - Process standardisation and procedure writing - Supplier qualification programmes - Quality audits

Cost: Low-medium; Returns: Very high

Costs to detect defects (inspection): - Material testing (concrete tests, weld inspections) - Site inspections and surveys - Software testing - Third-party audits and certifications - Equipment calibration

Cost: Medium; Returns: Medium-high

Defects found BEFORE delivery: - Rework (fixing defects before client sees them) - Scrap (material wasted due to errors) - Redesign required - Re-testing after rework

Cost: High; Avoided by prevention and appraisal

Defects found AFTER delivery: - Warranty claims and rectification - Legal liability - Lost future business - Regulatory fines - Brand damage - Property damage or personal injury

Cost: Extremely high; Avoided by good QA/QC

Warning⚠️ The 1-10-100 Rule of Quality

The cost of fixing a defect multiplies with how late it is discovered:

  • Cost to prevent (design stage): $1
  • Cost to detect and fix (during construction): $10
  • Cost to fix after handover (warranty claim): $100

This is why front-end investment in quality planning and design reviews has the highest ROI in engineering projects.

Case Study — Jeddah Floods 2009: The Hidden Cost of Quality Failure

Important🏭 Case Study: Jeddah Flooding — When Quality Assurance Is the First Budget Cut

Background:
On 25 November 2009, a single rainstorm struck Jeddah, Saudi Arabia — 90 mm of rain in 3 hours, equivalent to the city’s entire annual average rainfall in one event. The results were catastrophic: 161 officially confirmed deaths (independent estimates suggest over 500), thousands of vehicles swept away, and entire residential districts submerged under metres of water. The Saudi government subsequently spent over SAR 35 billion (~$9 billion) in disaster response and infrastructure reconstruction.

The engineering tragedy was not the rainfall — extreme flash floods are a known geological risk in the Hejaz region. The tragedy was the absence of drainage infrastructure that should have been built and inspected years earlier.

What the Post-Disaster Investigation Found:
A Saudi Control and Investigation Board inquiry revealed systematic quality management failure across decades of city infrastructure development:

Infrastructure Element Required Actually Built
Primary storm drainage network 500 km specified in master plan ~200 km built; much of it blocked by sediment and illegal connections
Retention ponds 15 major retention basins required 3 completed; 8 plot allocations reassigned to housing developers
Road drainage channels Minimum 600 mm kerb drainage on all major roads Most roads had 200–300 mm drainage; many had none
Construction inspections All drainage works inspected before roadway overlay Independent estimates: 60%+ of roads paved without drainage inspection sign-off

Quality Management Failures:

1. Paper-Based QA Without Field Verification:
Contractors routinely submitted quality inspection certificates for works that had not been completed to specification. No independent inspector challenged the certificates. The “quality assurance system” was form completion — not field verification.

2. Cost of Quality Inversion:
Prevention cost (installing the missing drainage correctly): estimated at SAR 1.3 billion.
Failure cost paid (disaster response, reconstruction, compensation): SAR 35+ billion.
This is a 27:1 ratio — a textbook demonstration of how eliminating quality investment creates catastrophic failure costs.

3. Conflict of Interest in QA Oversight:
The municipal authority, urban development office, and construction contractors were linked through business and family relationships — structural compromises to QA independence that allowed systemic non-conformance to persist undetected.

4. Accumulation of “Acceptable” Non-Conformances:
Individual drainage shortfalls in each development project were classified as “non-material NCRs” and signed off. Over 20 years of rapid city growth, thousands of individually approved NCRs accumulated into a completely broken citywide drainage system.

The True Cost of Quality — Jeddah 2009:

Cost Category Amount Notes
Emergency response SAR 4.8 billion Immediate
Road and drainage reconstruction SAR 6.2 billion Multi-year programme
Compensation to bereaved families SAR 0.3 billion
Lost business and private property SAR 12+ billion Estimated
Prevention cost if QA had functioned SAR 1.3 billion Full drainage system cost

Quality Lessons: 1. QA independence is not a luxury — when the inspector and contractor share financial interests, there is no quality assurance 2. The cost of preventing quality failure is always a fraction of the failure cost — quality is never the line to cut in a budget overrun 3. Individual NCRs accumulate systemic risk — no single non-conformance appears catastrophic in isolation; the pattern of non-conformances is 4. Paper-based QA systems are not quality systems — quality assurance requires field verification and independent sign-off, not form completion 5. Quality culture requires consequences — if contractors face no accountability for non-conformance, non-conformance will be universal

Discussion Questions: 1. Which quality management process (Planning, QA, or QC) failed most catastrophically in this case? 2. Design a quality audit programme that would have detected the drainage infrastructure failures before they became a crisis 3. How does this case illustrate the relationship between procurement governance, stakeholder independence, and quality outcomes in rapidly developing Arab cities?

Sources: Saudi Geological Survey (2010); Arab Fund for Economic and Social Development (2010)

## What is Quality in Engineering Projects? [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Two Dimensions of Quality

**Quality of Design (Fitness for Purpose):**  
Does the delivered product meet the intended purpose? Did we deliver what the client NEEDED?

**Quality of Conformance (Conformance to Specifications):**  
Was the product built exactly to the approved specifications, drawings, and standards?

*Example:* A bridge designed for 40-tonne trucks but only used by cars is HIGH quality of conformance (built as designed) but potentially POOR quality of design (over-engineered = waste). A bridge designed for 100-tonne loads but built with B-grade steel is HIGH quality of design but POOR quality of conformance.
:::

### Grade vs. Quality

| | High Grade | Low Grade |
|-|-----------|---------|
| **High Quality** | Premium product built to spec | Budget product built to spec |
| **Low Quality** | Premium product with defects | Budget product with defects |

*Grade is the level of performance requirements; Quality is whether those requirements are met.*

## 📖 QA vs. QC — The Critical Distinction

**Quality Assurance (QA):** Focuses on *processes* — "Are we doing the right things the right way?"
- Proactive and process-oriented
- Performed DURING the project
- Examples: Process audits, design reviews, peer checks, construction supervision

**Quality Control (QC):** Focuses on *outputs* — "Does the product meet requirements?"
- Reactive and product-oriented
- Applied to completed work
- Examples: Material testing, dimensional surveys, pressure testing, functional testing
:::

## The Quality Management Plan [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

A **Quality Management Plan (QMP)** defines how quality will be managed throughout the project:

::: {.panel-tabset}

## QMP Contents
1. **Quality objectives** — measurable quality targets linked to project scope
2. **Quality standards** — applicable codes, standards, specifications (e.g., AS/NZS, ISO, ASTM)
3. **Quality responsibilities** — who is accountable for quality activities
4. **Inspection and Test Plan (ITP)** — what will be tested, by whom, and when
5. **Non-conformance procedure** — how defects are identified, documented, and resolved
6. **Document control** — version control and distribution of quality documents
7. **Audit schedule** — when QA audits will be conducted

## Inspection and Test Plan (ITP)
The ITP is the core QC tool — it specifies every inspection and test:

| Activity | Test/Inspection | Standard | Frequency | Inspector | Hold/Witness/Review | Acceptance Criteria |
|----------|----------------|---------|---------|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Concrete pour | Slump test | AS 1012.3 | Each truck | Site Engineer | Hold (H) | 80-120mm |
| Concrete | 28-day cylinder test | AS 1012.9 | 1 set per 50m³ | NATA Lab | Review (R) | ≥ N32 (32MPa) |
| Structural steel weld | Visual inspection | AS/NZS 1554 | 100% | Inspector | Witness (W) | No visible defects |
| Steel weld | NDT (ultrasonic) | AS 2205 | 10% random | Certified UT tech | Review (R) | Class B |
| Pipeline hydrostatic test | Pressure test | AS 2885 | Each segment | Engineer | Witness (W) | 1.5× operating pressure, 1 hour |

**Hold/Witness/Review Codes:**
- **H = Hold Point:** Work CANNOT proceed without inspector present and signing off
- **W = Witness Point:** Inspector should attend; notified 24 hours in advance; can proceed if not present
- **R = Review:** Documentation reviewed by inspector; work can proceed

### 2. Pareto Analysis (80/20 Rule)

Pareto analysis identifies the **vital few causes** that account for most quality problems:

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Pareto Principle

> *"80% of problems come from 20% of causes."*

By focusing QC efforts on the 20% of causes driving 80% of defects, quality can be dramatically improved with limited resources.
:::

### 5. Scatter Diagram

A **scatter diagram** (scatter plot) helps determine whether a **relationship (correlation) exists between two variables**. The closer data points cluster around a diagonal line, the stronger the correlation.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Reading a Scatter Diagram

| Pattern | Interpretation |
|---------|---------------|
| Points cluster along rising diagonal | **Positive correlation** — as X increases, Y increases |
| Points cluster along falling diagonal | **Negative correlation** — as X increases, Y decreases |
| Points scattered randomly | **No correlation** — the variables are independent |

*Example: Plot concrete slump (X) vs. compressive strength (Y). A negative correlation would suggest higher water/cement ratio reduces strength.*
:::


### 6. Histogram

A **histogram** is a bar chart showing the **frequency distribution** of a quality variable — how many occurrences fall within each interval. It reveals the shape of a distribution: symmetric, skewed, or bimodal.

```{mermaid}
%%| fig-cap: "Histogram: distribution of concrete test results"
xychart-beta
    title "Concrete 28-Day Strength — Test Result Distribution"
    x-axis "Strength Range (MPa)" ["<30", "30-33", "33-36", "36-39", "39-42", "42-45", ">45"]
    y-axis "Frequency (test results)" 0 --> 18
    bar [1, 3, 8, 16, 12, 5, 2]
```

*The distribution is roughly bell-shaped, centred around 36–39 MPa. The one result below 30 MPa is an outlier requiring investigation.*

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Histogram vs. Bar Chart

A histogram shows the distribution of a **continuous variable** (like strength or temperature) grouped into ranges. A bar chart compares **distinct categories**. The bars in a histogram touch each other; bar chart bars have gaps.
:::

### 7. Run Chart

A **run chart** (also used for stratification) displays the **history and pattern of a process variable over time** — without control limits. Unlike control charts, run charts show trends and cycles that indicate non-random patterns.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 What to Look for in a Run Chart

- **Trend** — 7+ consecutive points consistently rising or falling → process is drifting
- **Run** — 8+ consecutive points on the same side of the median → process is not centred
- **Cycle** — repeating up-down pattern → likely shift-based or equipment-based variation
:::

### Statistical Sampling

**Statistical sampling** involves selecting a **representative subset** of items from a population for inspection — instead of testing every single item (100% inspection).

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Sample Size Formula

$$n = 0.25 \times \left(\frac{CF}{e}\right)^2$$

Where:
- $n$ = required sample size
- $CF$ = certainty factor (from standard table)
- $e$ = acceptable error rate (as a decimal)

| Desired Certainty | Certainty Factor (CF) |
|------------------|-----------------------|
| 90% | 1.65 |
| 95% | 1.96 |
| 98% | 2.33 |
| 99% | 2.58 |
:::

::: {.callout-tip icon="false"}
## 💡 Solved Example

You want to be **95% certain** that your sample result is within **±5%** of the true population value.

$$n = 0.25 \times \left(\frac{1.96}{0.05}\right)^2 = 0.25 \times (39.2)^2 = 0.25 \times 1536.64 \approx \mathbf{384}$$

You need a sample of **384 items**.

*If you can accept more error (±10%), the required sample size drops to 96. There is always a trade-off between precision and inspection cost.*
:::

**When to use statistical sampling:**
- Concrete cube testing (test a sample of pours, not every cubic metre)
- Software testing (test a representative sample of code paths)
- Incoming material inspection (test a sample from each batch)
- Customer satisfaction surveys (survey a sample of users)

## Performing Quality Assurance — Kaizen, Lean, and Kanban [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

**Quality Assurance (QA)** includes all activities related to satisfying quality standards. Beyond audits and process checks, modern QA embraces **continuous improvement philosophies**:

### Kaizen (改善)

**Kaizen** is the Japanese philosophy of **continuous incremental improvement** — every employee, at every level, contributes small improvements every day.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Kaizen Principles

1. **Go to Gemba** — observe the actual work being done; improvements come from the field, not the office
2. **Eliminate waste (Muda)** — identify and remove activities that add no value
3. **Standardise then improve** — you cannot improve what is not standardised
4. **Small steps, not big leaps** — many small improvements are more sustainable than occasional breakthroughs
5. **Everyone participates** — quality improvement is not just the QA department's job

*Example in engineering: A site team notices that waiting for RFI responses causes an average 2-day delay per week. Kaizen event: team designs a 24-hour RFI response target with escalation — rework and delays drop by 60%.*
:::

### Lean

**Lean** focuses on **maximising customer value while minimising waste**. Lean evaluates every activity as either **value-adding** or **waste** (non-value-adding):

| Lean Waste Category | Description | Engineering Example |
|--------------------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Overproduction** | Producing more than needed | Drawing revisions not yet needed |
| **Waiting** | Idle time while waiting for inputs | Waiting for material delivery, approval |
| **Transport** | Unnecessary movement of materials | Moving equipment across site unnecessarily |
| **Over-processing** | More work than required | Preparing 100-page reports when 2 pages needed |
| **Inventory** | Excess materials or work in progress | Too much concrete on site; unprocessed RFIs |
| **Motion** | Unnecessary movement of people | Walking to a distant printer 20×/day |
| **Defects** | Rework and rectification | Concrete pour requiring full demolition |

*Lean PM projects that eliminate waste achieve 20–40% reductions in project duration and cost.*

### Kanban

**Kanban** is a visual workflow management system that uses a **Kanban board** to limit work-in-progress (WIP) and improve flow:

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Kanban Core Properties

1. **Visualise the workflow** — all tasks visible on a board (To Do / In Progress / Review / Done)
2. **Limit WIP (Work in Progress)** — each column has a maximum number of items; prevents overloading
3. **Manage flow** — measure how long tasks take to move through the system (cycle time)
4. **Make process policies explicit** — everyone agrees on what "Done" means for each column
5. **Identify improvement opportunities** — bottlenecks become visible when WIP limits are hit

*Kanban originated in Toyota manufacturing (1940s) and is now widely used in software development, design teams, and construction project offices.*

**How Kanban differs from Scrum:** Kanban is flow-based (continuous delivery); Scrum is iteration-based (fixed-length sprints). Kanban does not have defined roles or ceremonies — it is applied to the existing workflow.
:::

## Six Sigma [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

**Six Sigma** is a comprehensive, data-driven quality methodology that aims to **reduce defects to no more than 3.4 per million opportunities** — achieving near-perfect quality.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Six Sigma — Basic Facts

| Item | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Goal** | ≤ 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO) |
| **Statistical basis** | A process at 6σ means the nearest specification limit is 6 standard deviations from the mean |
| **Origin** | Motorola (1986); popularised by General Electric (Jack Welch, 1990s) |
| **Driven by** | Customer needs, facts and data, statistical analysis |
| **Applies to** | Manufacturing, IT, healthcare, construction, services |
:::

### The DMAIC Improvement Cycle

Six Sigma projects follow the **DMAIC** framework:


### The Six Sigma Belt System

Six Sigma organisations train practitioners at different levels:

| Belt | Role | Responsibilities |
|------|------|-----------------|
| **White Belt** | Team member | Basic awareness; supports projects |
| **Yellow Belt** | Team contributor | Participates in improvement projects |
| **Green Belt** | Part-time project leader | Leads small improvement projects alongside regular duties |
| **Black Belt** | Full-time project leader | Leads major DMAIC projects; mentors Green Belts |
| **Master Black Belt** | Programme leader | Coaches Black Belts; drives organisational strategy |
| **Champion** | Executive sponsor | Selects and sponsors Six Sigma projects; removes barriers |

### Six Sigma and Statistics

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Standard Deviation and Sigma Levels

The term **sigma (σ)** is the standard deviation — a measure of process variability. The sigma level indicates how many standard deviations fit between the process mean and the nearest specification limit:

| Sigma Level | DPMO | Quality (%) |
|-------------|------|-------------|
| 1σ | 691,462 | 30.9% |
| 2σ | 308,538 | 69.1% |
| 3σ | 66,807 | 93.3% |
| 4σ | 6,210 | 99.4% |
| 5σ | 233 | 99.98% |
| **6σ** | **3.4** | **99.9997%** |

*Most industries operate at 3σ–4σ. Airlines achieve ~6σ for fatal accident rates; hospital medication error rates are often at 2σ–3σ.*
:::

$$\text{At 6σ: } P(\text{defect}) = 3.4 \times 10^{-6}$$

### Six Sigma and Project Management

Six Sigma projects are managed like projects:
- **Business case and project charter** — defines scope, goals, and team
- **Schedule and budget** — tracked using PM tools
- **Team structure** — Black Belt as project manager; Champion as sponsor
- **Stage-gate reviews** — approval at each DMAIC phase before proceeding
- **Benefits realisation** — financial savings tracked and reported after Control phase

## Software Testing [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

Testing is a critical QC activity for IT and software projects. **Testing alone cannot guarantee quality** — it must be part of a broader QA strategy — but it is an essential defect-detection mechanism.

### Types of Tests

::: {.panel-tabset}

## Unit Testing
**Tests individual functions or modules in isolation**

- Performed by developers during coding
- Uses test frameworks (JUnit, pytest, NUnit)
- Verifies: Does this function do what it is supposed to do?
- Finds bugs early, when they are cheapest to fix
- Target: 80%+ code coverage for critical systems

## Integration Testing
**Tests how modules/components work together**

- Performed after unit testing
- Finds interface defects (data passed incorrectly between modules)
- Approaches: Top-down, bottom-up, big-bang
- Verifies: Do the connected parts communicate correctly?

## System Testing
**Tests the complete, integrated system against requirements**

- Black-box testing — tests behaviour, not internal code
- Covers: functional requirements, performance, security, usability
- Performed in an environment that mirrors production
- The primary stage for finding defects before user acceptance

## User Acceptance Testing (UAT)
**End users test the system against real business scenarios**

- Final stage before go-live
- Users confirm the system meets their needs
- Not about finding bugs — about confirming fitness for purpose
- A failed UAT does not always mean the system is broken; sometimes requirements were wrong

## Regression Testing
**Re-runs existing tests after changes to verify nothing was broken**

- Run after every bug fix or new feature
- Automated regression suites are essential for large systems
- Prevents the "fix one thing, break two others" problem

## Performance Testing
**Tests system behaviour under load**

- Load testing: normal expected load
- Stress testing: beyond expected load (find breaking point)
- Spike testing: sudden traffic increases
- Endurance testing: sustained load over long period

:::

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Testing Alone Is Not Enough

> *"Testing shows the presence of bugs, not their absence."* — Edsger Dijkstra

Testing finds defects **after** they exist. The most cost-effective strategy is to prevent defects through:
- Clear, unambiguous requirements
- Design reviews and peer inspections
- Coding standards and code reviews
- Continuous integration (automated testing on every commit)

The **1-10-100 rule** applies: A requirement defect costs $1 to fix in design; $10 in development; $100 in production.
:::

## Quality Experts [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

Several pioneers shaped modern quality management thinking. Every project manager should know their key contributions:

::: {.panel-tabset}

## W. Edwards Deming

**W. Edwards Deming** (1900–1993) — American statistician and management consultant; widely credited with Japan's quality revolution post-WWII.

**Key contributions:**
- **PDCA Cycle** (Plan-Do-Check-Act) — the foundation of continuous improvement
- **14 Points for Management** — a framework for transforming organisational quality culture
- **System of Profound Knowledge** — understanding systems, variation, psychology, and theory of knowledge
- **Emphasis on management's role**: Deming believed 85% of quality problems are management problems, not worker problems

*Famous quote: "In God we trust; all others bring data."*

## Joseph M. Juran

**Joseph M. Juran** (1904–2008) — Romanian-born American engineer and quality expert.

**Key contributions:**
- **Quality Trilogy**: Quality Planning → Quality Control → Quality Improvement
- **Pareto Principle applied to quality**: 80% of problems from 20% of causes (the "vital few")
- **Fitness for use**: Quality is not just conformance to spec — the product must be fit for its intended purpose
- **"All improvement takes place project by project"** — quality improvement requires project discipline

*Juran's Quality Handbook is considered the bible of quality management.*

## Philip Crosby

**Philip Crosby** (1926–2001) — American businessman and quality consultant.

**Key contributions:**
- **Zero Defects** concept — the performance standard should be doing things right the first time, every time
- **Quality is Free** (1979 book) — argued that the cost of prevention is always less than the cost of failures
- **Four Absolutes of Quality Management**:
  1. Quality is conformance to requirements (not goodness)
  2. The system of quality is prevention (not appraisal)
  3. The performance standard is Zero Defects (not AQL)
  4. The measurement of quality is the Price of Non-conformance (not indices)
- **14 Steps to Quality Improvement** — a practical implementation programme

## Kaoru Ishikawa

**Kaoru Ishikawa** (1915–1989) — Japanese chemical engineer; pioneer of quality circles.

**Key contributions:**
- **Fishbone (Ishikawa) Diagram** — cause-and-effect diagram for root cause analysis
- **Quality Circles** — small groups of workers who meet regularly to identify and solve quality problems in their own work
- **Company-wide quality control (CWQC)** — quality is everyone's responsibility, not just the QA department
- **Seven Basic Tools of Quality**: Cause-and-effect diagram, checksheet, control chart, histogram, Pareto chart, scatter diagram, flowchart/run chart

*Ishikawa popularised the idea that front-line workers, given the right tools and training, can solve most quality problems themselves.*

## Cost of Quality [📋 Required]{.badge-req}

The **Cost of Quality (CoQ)** model recognises that ALL quality activities have a cost — and that investing in prevention is far cheaper than paying for failures:

::: {.panel-tabset}

## Prevention Costs
**Costs to prevent defects (proactive):**
- Quality planning and QMP development
- Staff training and competency assessment
- Design reviews and peer checks
- Process standardisation and procedure writing
- Supplier qualification programmes
- Quality audits

*Cost: Low-medium; Returns: Very high*

## Appraisal Costs
**Costs to detect defects (inspection):**
- Material testing (concrete tests, weld inspections)
- Site inspections and surveys
- Software testing
- Third-party audits and certifications
- Equipment calibration

*Cost: Medium; Returns: Medium-high*

## Internal Failure Costs
**Defects found BEFORE delivery:**
- Rework (fixing defects before client sees them)
- Scrap (material wasted due to errors)
- Redesign required
- Re-testing after rework

*Cost: High; Avoided by prevention and appraisal*

## External Failure Costs
**Defects found AFTER delivery:**
- Warranty claims and rectification
- Legal liability
- Lost future business
- Regulatory fines
- Brand damage
- Property damage or personal injury

*Cost: Extremely high; Avoided by good QA/QC*

:::

::: {.callout-warning icon="false"}
## ⚠️ The 1-10-100 Rule of Quality

The cost of fixing a defect multiplies with how late it is discovered:

- **Cost to prevent** (design stage): **$1**
- **Cost to detect and fix** (during construction): **$10**
- **Cost to fix after handover** (warranty claim): **$100**

This is why front-end investment in quality planning and design reviews has the highest ROI in engineering projects.
:::

## Case Study — Jeddah Floods 2009: The Hidden Cost of Quality Failure {.unnumbered}

::: {.callout-important icon="false"}
## 🏭 Case Study: Jeddah Flooding — When Quality Assurance Is the First Budget Cut

**Background:**  
On 25 November 2009, a single rainstorm struck Jeddah, Saudi Arabia — **90 mm of rain in 3 hours**, equivalent to the city's entire annual average rainfall in one event. The results were catastrophic: **161 officially confirmed deaths** (independent estimates suggest over 500), thousands of vehicles swept away, and entire residential districts submerged under metres of water. The Saudi government subsequently spent over **SAR 35 billion (~$9 billion)** in disaster response and infrastructure reconstruction.

The engineering tragedy was not the rainfall — extreme flash floods are a known geological risk in the Hejaz region. **The tragedy was the absence of drainage infrastructure that should have been built and inspected years earlier.**

**What the Post-Disaster Investigation Found:**  
A Saudi Control and Investigation Board inquiry revealed systematic quality management failure across decades of city infrastructure development:

| Infrastructure Element | Required | Actually Built |
|----------------------|---------|---------------|
| Primary storm drainage network | 500 km specified in master plan | ~200 km built; much of it blocked by sediment and illegal connections |
| Retention ponds | 15 major retention basins required | 3 completed; 8 plot allocations reassigned to housing developers |
| Road drainage channels | Minimum 600 mm kerb drainage on all major roads | Most roads had 200–300 mm drainage; many had none |
| Construction inspections | All drainage works inspected before roadway overlay | Independent estimates: 60%+ of roads paved without drainage inspection sign-off |

**Quality Management Failures:**

*1. Paper-Based QA Without Field Verification:*  
Contractors routinely submitted quality inspection certificates for works that had not been completed to specification. No independent inspector challenged the certificates. The "quality assurance system" was form completion — not field verification.

*2. Cost of Quality Inversion:*  
Prevention cost (installing the missing drainage correctly): estimated at SAR 1.3 billion.  
Failure cost paid (disaster response, reconstruction, compensation): **SAR 35+ billion**.  
This is a **27:1 ratio** — a textbook demonstration of how eliminating quality investment creates catastrophic failure costs.

*3. Conflict of Interest in QA Oversight:*  
The municipal authority, urban development office, and construction contractors were linked through business and family relationships — structural compromises to QA independence that allowed systemic non-conformance to persist undetected.

*4. Accumulation of "Acceptable" Non-Conformances:*  
Individual drainage shortfalls in each development project were classified as "non-material NCRs" and signed off. Over 20 years of rapid city growth, thousands of individually approved NCRs accumulated into a completely broken citywide drainage system.

**The True Cost of Quality — Jeddah 2009:**

| Cost Category | Amount | Notes |
|--------------|--------|-------|
| Emergency response | SAR 4.8 billion | Immediate |
| Road and drainage reconstruction | SAR 6.2 billion | Multi-year programme |
| Compensation to bereaved families | SAR 0.3 billion | |
| Lost business and private property | SAR 12+ billion | Estimated |
| **Prevention cost if QA had functioned** | **SAR 1.3 billion** | *Full drainage system cost* |

**Quality Lessons:**
1. **QA independence is not a luxury** — when the inspector and contractor share financial interests, there is no quality assurance
2. **The cost of preventing quality failure is always a fraction of the failure cost** — quality is never the line to cut in a budget overrun
3. **Individual NCRs accumulate systemic risk** — no single non-conformance appears catastrophic in isolation; the pattern of non-conformances is
4. **Paper-based QA systems are not quality systems** — quality assurance requires field verification and independent sign-off, not form completion
5. **Quality culture requires consequences** — if contractors face no accountability for non-conformance, non-conformance will be universal

**Discussion Questions:**
1. Which quality management process (Planning, QA, or QC) failed most catastrophically in this case?
2. Design a quality audit programme that would have detected the drainage infrastructure failures before they became a crisis
3. How does this case illustrate the relationship between procurement governance, stakeholder independence, and quality outcomes in rapidly developing Arab cities?

*Sources: @jeddah2009floods; @arab2010infra*
:::
