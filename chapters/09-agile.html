Adaptive project management for uncertain, complex engineering challenges — Agile principles, Scrum framework, and hybrid approaches.

13.1 The Agile Mindset

Traditional (Waterfall) project management assumes that requirements can be fully defined at the start and that the best approach is to plan completely before executing.

Agile was born from the recognition that many projects — especially in software, R&D, and innovative engineering — operate in environments where:

  • Requirements evolve and change
  • The problem is not fully understood at the start
  • Frequent feedback from users is more valuable than following a fixed plan
  • Early delivery of partial value is better than waiting for complete delivery
Note📖 The Agile Manifesto (2001)

The Agile Manifesto (Beck et al. 2001) states four core values:

We value… …over
Individuals and interactions Processes and tools
Working software Comprehensive documentation
Customer collaboration Contract negotiation
Responding to change Following a plan

“That is, while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more.”

Note: Agile does NOT say “no documentation” or “no planning” — it says the items on the LEFT are prioritised when they conflict with the items on the RIGHT.

13.2 The Scrum Framework

Scrum is the most widely used Agile framework. It organises work into short, time-boxed iterations called Sprints:

13.2.1 The Three Scrum Roles

  • Product Owner
  • Scrum Master
  • Development Team

Maximises value of the product/project

Responsibilities: - Maintains and prioritises the Product Backlog - Ensures the team understands what needs to be built AND why - Accepts or rejects sprint output (is it “done”?) - Represents the voice of the customer/user/stakeholder - Has authority to make product decisions

In Engineering Context:
The Product Owner might be the client’s lead engineer, the project sponsor’s representative, or a senior PM who can make real-time scope decisions.

Guardian of the Scrum process

Responsibilities: - Facilitates all Scrum ceremonies (planning, daily scrum, review, retrospective) - Removes impediments (blockers that prevent the team from working) - Coaches the team on Scrum practices - Shields the team from external interruptions during sprints - NOT a traditional PM — does not assign tasks or manage people

Important: The Scrum Master serves the team, not the other way around.

Self-organising, cross-functional group that builds the product

Characteristics: - Self-organising: Team decides HOW to do the work; no sub-teams - Cross-functional: All skills needed to complete a sprint are within the team - Accountable: Team owns delivery of the sprint goal, not individuals - Size: Typically 3-9 people (smaller = more agile; larger = more communication overhead)

In Engineering Context:
Might include mechanical engineers, software developers, test engineers, and documentation specialists — all working together within the sprint rather than in separate sequential phases.

13.2.3 Scrum Artefacts

  • Product Backlog
  • Sprint Backlog
  • Increment

Prioritised list of EVERYTHING that might need to be done

Written as User Stories:

“As a [user type], I want to [do something], so that [I achieve some benefit].”

Engineering User Story Examples:

# User Story Story Points Priority
1 As a system operator, I want to view real-time pump status on a dashboard, so that I can quickly identify failures 8 High
2 As a maintenance technician, I want to receive automated alerts when vibration exceeds thresholds, so that I can prevent bearing failures 13 High
3 As an engineer, I want to export historical data to CSV, so that I can analyse trends in Excel 3 Medium
4 As a manager, I want a weekly energy consumption report, so that I can track efficiency 5 Medium

The subset of the Product Backlog committed to in the current sprint

The team selects items it can complete within the sprint based on: - Velocity (average story points completed per sprint, from historical data) - Sprint Goal (the objective of this specific sprint) - Capacity (team availability, accounting for leave, meetings, etc.)

A Sprint Backlog item should be completable within one sprint. If too large, it is decomposed into smaller tasks.

The sum of all completed Product Backlog items from all previous sprints

Must be: - Potentially shippable (meets the “Definition of Done”) - Valuable to stakeholders - Demonstrable at the Sprint Review

Definition of Done (DoD): A shared understanding of what “completed” means. For each story/feature: - Code written and reviewed (for software) - Tests written and passing - Documentation updated - Stakeholder accepted

For Engineering: DoD might include design calculations checked, drawings peer-reviewed, and client acceptance received.

13.2.4 The Burndown Chart

A sprint burndown chart tracks progress towards completing the sprint backlog:

Ideal line (straight diagonal): Team needs to complete 4 points/day.
Actual line: Team is slightly behind Days 1-5, but catches up by end.

13.3 Kanban

Kanban is a visual workflow management method that limits work-in-progress to improve flow:

Key Kanban Principles: 1. Visualise the work 2. Limit Work-In-Progress (WIP) — prevents multitasking and identifies bottlenecks 3. Manage flow — optimise the movement of work through the system 4. Make process policies explicit — everyone knows the rules 5. Implement feedback loops — regular reviews of the system 6. Improve collaboratively — continuous, incremental improvements

13.4 Waterfall vs. Agile — Choosing the Right Approach

Factor Waterfall Agile
Requirements Fixed at start Evolve throughout
Deliverables Complete product at end Working increments each sprint
Change Controlled, costly to change Expected and welcome
Client involvement At start and end Continuous
Documentation Comprehensive Lightweight, just enough
Risk High (late discovery) Low (early feedback)
Best for Stable, well-defined projects Innovative, complex, uncertain
Examples Bridge construction, plant construction Software, R&D, digital systems

13.4.1 Hybrid Approaches for Engineering

Most large engineering projects use a hybrid approach:

Case Study — Agile in the Arab World: Amman Smart Traffic Management

Important🏭 Case Study: Amman Adaptive Traffic Control — Jordan’s First Agile Public Infrastructure Delivery

Background:
Greater Amman Municipality (GAM) contracted a Jordanian-European consortium in 2018 to deliver an Adaptive Traffic Control System (ATCS) across 350 signalised intersections in central Amman. The Ministry of Public Works had previously attempted a similar system in 2012 using a traditional waterfall approach — that $6.2 million contract was cancelled after 18 months with zero intersections operational.

This time, GAM insisted on an Agile delivery framework — a first for Jordan’s public sector infrastructure.

The Challenge:
An ATCS is never “finished” in the traditional sense. Requirements were incompletely understood at project start, Amman’s road network was evolving (3 new road tunnels under simultaneous construction), traffic data was sparse, and 15 stakeholder departments (traffic police, urban planning, emergency services, bus operators, Civil Defence) had conflicting priorities.

Agile Adoption:

1. Scrum for Software and Algorithm Development: - 2-week sprints for traffic algorithm development, CCTV integration, and the control dashboard - Product backlog maintained by a GAM Traffic Engineer acting as Product Owner — a government employee, not a consultant - Daily standups conducted in Arabic; sprint reviews included representatives from Amman Traffic Police - Definition of “Done” required each intersection to pass a 48-hour silent monitoring trial before being counted complete

2. Traditional Engineering for Physical Infrastructure: - Cabinet installation, fibre cable laying, and inductive detector loops retained standard engineering approval workflows - Physical works were batched into “packages” aligned with each sprint’s software scope — a genuine Hybrid delivery model

3. Sprint Cadence: - Sprints 1–4: Core system architecture, 10-intersection pilot (Downtown Amman), baseline traffic data collection - Sprints 5–9: Adaptive algorithm v1.0 deployed across 80 intersections; emergency vehicle pre-emption logic - Sprints 10–16: Corridor coordination (5 major arterials linking central districts); public information displays; GAM staff training - Sprints 17+: Expansion programme, remote monitoring portal, Ministry of Interior integration

Results: - First 10 intersections operational within 6 weeks of contract start (previously 18 months with zero result under waterfall) - 350 intersections fully integrated within 18 months, on schedule and within 3% of budget - Average peak-hour journey time on the Al-Medina Street corridor reduced by 22% in Year 1 - Client satisfaction: 8.7/10; GAM extended the contract for 400 additional intersections before the original scope was complete - The Arabic-language system interface and Arabic training materials were embedded from Sprint 1 — not added as an afterthought

Key Agile Lessons for Arab Engineers: 1. Deliver something working as early as possible — GAM saw 10 live intersections in Week 6; this rebuilt institutional trust that the 2012 failure had destroyed 2. Agile works in Arabic — the Scrum framework adapts to any language or cultural context; the Product Owner role was equally effective in a government setting 3. Hybrid is the norm in infrastructure — physical works followed standard engineering governance; algorithm and software development used Agile 4. Local ownership is critical — embedding a GAM engineer as Product Owner (not a consultant) ensured the system matched real operational needs, not theoretical requirements 5. Early failure is cheap — the 10-intersection pilot failed three times before the algorithm was tuned correctly; those failures cost days, not months

Caution✏️ Exercise 9.1 — Product Backlog Creation

A water utility wants to develop a Smart Water Network Monitoring System with the following desired features (from stakeholder workshops):

  1. Real-time pressure monitoring at 50 pressure points
  2. Automated leak detection using acoustic sensors
  3. Demand forecasting model (7-day ahead)
  4. Mobile app for field teams to access network status
  5. Data integration with existing SCADA system
  6. Alert generation when pressure drops below threshold
  7. Historical data archive (5 years)
  8. Customer-facing dashboard showing service status
  9. Asset management database linking sensors to assets
  10. Automated regulatory reporting (daily, monthly)
  11. Energy optimisation algorithm for pump scheduling
  12. Remote valve control capability

Task: 1. Write each feature as a properly formatted user story 2. Prioritise the backlog using MoSCoW (Must/Should/Could/Won’t for MVP) 3. Assign relative story point estimates (use Fibonacci: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20) 4. Assuming a team velocity of 30 points/sprint (2-week sprints), plan the first 3 sprints

Caution✏️ Exercise 9.2 — Sprint Planning and Burndown

Your Scrum team has a velocity of 35 story points per 2-week sprint. Sprint 4 starts with the following items selected:

Story Points Day work starts
Sensor API integration 8 Day 1
Pressure alert logic 5 Day 1
Dashboard layout component 8 Day 2
Unit tests for API 3 Day 3
Database schema for alerts 5 Day 4
User acceptance test plan 3 Day 5
End-to-end test: alert to notification 3 Day 8

Assuming one story is completed per working day (in order): 1. Create a sprint burndown chart (daily) showing actual progress 2. Draw the ideal burndown line on the same chart 3. By Day 5, are you on track? Explain using the burndown data. 4. What would you discuss in the Day 5 Scrum to address any issues?

Caution✏️ Exercise 9.3 — Agile vs. Waterfall Selection

For each of the following engineering projects, recommend either Waterfall, Agile, or a Hybrid approach. Justify your answer in 3-4 sentences.

  1. Construction of a 200m concrete arch dam in a remote location
  2. Development of embedded firmware for a new structural health monitoring sensor
  3. Detailed design of a wastewater treatment plant upgrade (known technology, defined specifications)
  4. Development of a smart city traffic management system (first of its kind, evolving requirements)
  5. Design and construction of a large underground metro station
  6. An R&D project to develop a new low-carbon concrete mix
  7. Annual maintenance shutdown and upgrade of a chemical manufacturing plant
  8. Development of an AI-powered geotechnical risk prediction tool
Caution✏️ Exercise 9.4 — Kanban Board Design

An engineering team of 6 people is managing the design development for a commercial building. The design process involves: Brief, Concept Design, Schematic Design, Design Development, and Documentation phases.

  1. Design a Kanban board with appropriate columns for this workflow
  2. Set WIP limits for each column (justify your limits)
  3. Identify 3 types of “work items” that would flow through this board
  4. How would you handle urgent items that arrive mid-week (interrupt tasks)?
  5. The team notices that items are piling up in the “Review” column. What does this tell you, and what would you do?

## Case Study — Agile in the Arab World: Amman Smart Traffic Management {.unnumbered}

::: {.callout-important icon="false"}
## 🏭 Case Study: Amman Adaptive Traffic Control — Jordan's First Agile Public Infrastructure Delivery

**Background:**  
Greater Amman Municipality (GAM) contracted a Jordanian-European consortium in 2018 to deliver an **Adaptive Traffic Control System (ATCS)** across 350 signalised intersections in central Amman. The Ministry of Public Works had previously attempted a similar system in 2012 using a traditional waterfall approach — that $6.2 million contract was cancelled after 18 months with zero intersections operational.

This time, GAM insisted on an **Agile delivery framework** — a first for Jordan's public sector infrastructure.

**The Challenge:**  
An ATCS is never "finished" in the traditional sense. Requirements were incompletely understood at project start, Amman's road network was evolving (3 new road tunnels under simultaneous construction), traffic data was sparse, and 15 stakeholder departments (traffic police, urban planning, emergency services, bus operators, Civil Defence) had conflicting priorities.

**Agile Adoption:**

*1. Scrum for Software and Algorithm Development:*
- 2-week sprints for traffic algorithm development, CCTV integration, and the control dashboard
- Product backlog maintained by a GAM Traffic Engineer acting as Product Owner — a government employee, not a consultant
- Daily standups conducted in Arabic; sprint reviews included representatives from Amman Traffic Police
- Definition of "Done" required each intersection to pass a 48-hour silent monitoring trial before being counted complete

*2. Traditional Engineering for Physical Infrastructure:*
- Cabinet installation, fibre cable laying, and inductive detector loops retained standard engineering approval workflows
- Physical works were batched into "packages" aligned with each sprint's software scope — a genuine Hybrid delivery model

*3. Sprint Cadence:*
- Sprints 1–4: Core system architecture, 10-intersection pilot (Downtown Amman), baseline traffic data collection
- Sprints 5–9: Adaptive algorithm v1.0 deployed across 80 intersections; emergency vehicle pre-emption logic
- Sprints 10–16: Corridor coordination (5 major arterials linking central districts); public information displays; GAM staff training
- Sprints 17+: Expansion programme, remote monitoring portal, Ministry of Interior integration

**Results:**
- First **10 intersections operational within 6 weeks** of contract start (previously 18 months with zero result under waterfall)
- 350 intersections fully integrated within **18 months**, on schedule and within 3% of budget
- Average peak-hour journey time on the Al-Medina Street corridor reduced by **22%** in Year 1
- Client satisfaction: **8.7/10**; GAM extended the contract for 400 additional intersections before the original scope was complete
- The Arabic-language system interface and Arabic training materials were embedded from Sprint 1 — not added as an afterthought

**Key Agile Lessons for Arab Engineers:**
1. **Deliver something working as early as possible** — GAM saw 10 live intersections in Week 6; this rebuilt institutional trust that the 2012 failure had destroyed
2. **Agile works in Arabic** — the Scrum framework adapts to any language or cultural context; the Product Owner role was equally effective in a government setting
3. **Hybrid is the norm in infrastructure** — physical works followed standard engineering governance; algorithm and software development used Agile
4. **Local ownership is critical** — embedding a GAM engineer as Product Owner (not a consultant) ensured the system matched real operational needs, not theoretical requirements
5. **Early failure is cheap** — the 10-intersection pilot failed three times before the algorithm was tuned correctly; those failures cost days, not months
