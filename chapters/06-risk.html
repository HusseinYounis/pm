Risk Management & Project Audit

Systematically identifying, analysing, and responding to threats and opportunities — and formally auditing project health to keep projects on track.

Note🔖 Chapter Status

All sections in this chapter are ➕ Extended content — topics beyond the required source materials (Schwalbe ITPM Ch.1–8), included to provide a complete engineering project management curriculum.

Learning Outcomes

Tip🎯 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Define risk and explain the difference between threats and opportunities
  2. Identify risks using structured techniques (brainstorming, FMEA, checklists)
  3. Perform qualitative risk analysis using a probability/impact matrix
  4. Perform quantitative risk analysis using Expected Monetary Value
  5. Develop appropriate risk response strategies (for threats and opportunities)
  6. Maintain a Risk Register throughout the project lifecycle
  7. Monitor risks and implement responses during project execution
  8. Conduct a project audit and prepare an audit report with health traffic-light rating

12.1 What is Risk?

Note📖 PMI Definition of Risk

“An uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on one or more project objectives such as scope, schedule, cost, or quality.” — PMBOK® Guide, 7th Edition

Key Insight: Risk ≠ Problem. A risk is a possibility, not a certainty. When a risk occurs, it becomes an issue that must be managed.

12.1.1 Threats vs. Opportunities

Risk can be negative (threats) or positive (opportunities):

Threats Opportunities
Definition Negative uncertainty Positive uncertainty
Goal Reduce probability and/or impact Increase probability and/or impact
Response strategies Avoid, Transfer, Mitigate, Accept Exploit, Share, Enhance, Accept
Examples Unexpected ground conditions, key person leaving, supply chain delays Early completion bonus, new technology reduces costs, regulatory approval faster than expected

12.2 Risk Identification

Risk identification is an ongoing, collaborative process — not a one-time event at project start.

12.2.1 Risk Identification Techniques

  • Brainstorming
  • Risk Checklists
  • FMEA / HAZID
  • SWOT Analysis
  • Assumption and Constraint Analysis

Free-form group ideation in a risk workshop

Process: 1. Assemble cross-functional team (PM, engineers, client rep, external specialist) 2. Use structured prompts: “What could go wrong with…?” 3. Capture all ideas without judgment 4. Categorise and de-duplicate 5. Assess each identified risk

Tips: - Use an experienced facilitator, not the PM (who might inhibit responses) - Time-box: 30-60 minutes per major project phase - Anonymous input methods (sticky notes, digital tools) encourage candid responses

Use historical risk lists from similar projects

Industry-specific checklists exist for: - Civil/structural engineering - Process plant design - IT systems implementation - Environmental projects - Offshore/subsea projects

Checklists ensure common risks are not overlooked but must be supplemented with project-specific risks.

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis / Hazard Identification

Used in engineering and safety-critical industries:

  1. For each component/process, ask: “How can this FAIL?”
  2. For each failure mode: “What is the EFFECT of this failure?”
  3. Assign: Severity, Occurrence probability, Detectability
  4. Calculate Risk Priority Number (RPN): \[RPN = Severity \times Occurrence \times Detectability\]
  5. Focus on highest RPN items

Structured analysis of project context

Positive (enhance) Negative (address)
Internal Strengths Weaknesses
External Opportunities Threats

Useful for early risk identification and strategic risk assessment. Connect SWOT factors to specific risk scenarios.

Challenge every assumption in the project plan

For each assumption: “What happens if this assumption is WRONG?”

Examples: - “We assumed excavation would encounter class 3 material — what if it’s class 5?” - “We assumed the supplier could deliver in 8 weeks — what if it takes 14?” - “We assumed EPA approval within 6 months — what if it takes 12?”

12.4 Quantitative Risk Analysis

Quantitative Risk Analysis assigns numerical values to risks, allowing financial exposure and schedule uncertainty to be modelled.

12.4.1 Expected Monetary Value (EMV)

\[EMV = Probability \times Impact\ (\$)\]

Example Risk Register with EMV:

Risk ID Risk Description Probability Impact (\() | EMV (\)) Response
R-001 Acid sulphate soils discovered 30% $350,000 $105,000 Mitigation plan
R-002 Steel supplier delays 6 weeks 25% $180,000 $45,000 Alternative supplier
R-003 Client scope additions 60% $120,000 $72,000 Change control
R-004 Regulatory approval extended 20% $250,000 $50,000 Early pre-lodgement
R-005 Favourable ground conditions 40% -$80,000 -$32,000 Exploit opportunity
Total EMV $240,000

The total EMV of $240,000 should be included in the project contingency fund.

Case Study — Mecca Crane Collapse 2015: The Cost of Ignored Risk

Important🏭 Case Study: Masjid al-Haram Crane Collapse — Risk Management Under Deadline Pressure

Background:
On 11 September 2015 — just 11 days before Hajj was due to begin — a Liebherr LR 11350 crane collapsed at the Masjid al-Haram (Grand Mosque of Mecca) construction site during a storm. The crane fell into the mosque’s northern courtyard, directly onto pilgrims performing prayers. The collapse killed 111 people and injured 394 others.

Saudi Binladin Group (SBG), one of the world’s largest construction contractors and the Kingdom’s most prestigious builder, was managing the largest ongoing expansion of Islam’s holiest site.

Risk Management Failures:

Risk Factor What Should Have Happened What Actually Happened
Crane securing protocol Cranes must be secured in travel (free-slew) mode before storms; standards require de-rigging when wind exceeds 72 km/h Wind reached 83 km/h; the crane remained rigged and unsecured in travel mode
Meteorological monitoring 48-hour advance weather monitoring with automatic crane protocols triggered at defined wind thresholds A weather alert was issued but not formally communicated to crane operators
Public exclusion zones Live construction adjacent to an occupied worship space — an extraordinary risk context requiring enhanced protocols No enhanced exclusion zone protocol existed for public prayer areas below active cranes during extreme weather
Equipment condition All cranes subject to regular third-party certification Investigation indicated the crane’s luffing jib pin was in a degraded condition — a known maintenance concern
Risk culture Supervisors must have authority to halt work for any safety concern The 11-day countdown to Hajj created “normalisation of deviance” — routine shortcuts had accumulated over weeks without incident

The Normalisation of Deviance:
SBG had worked under intense Hajj-season deadline pressure for decades without a major fatal incident. Shortcuts that deviated from formal safety protocols had become routine because “nothing bad had happened before.” The 2015 collapse demonstrates that statistical tolerance of risk does not reduce the underlying probability of catastrophe — it merely delays it.

Financial and Operational Consequences: - Criminal investigation of SBG management; 13 individuals charged - Following the crane collapse and a separate Mina pilgrimage stampede (24 September 2015), SBG was suspended from all Saudi government contracts by end of 2015 - SBG had revenues exceeding $5 billion/year — the suspension was an existential business event - Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 subsequently restructured the Haramain and Haram construction contracting model entirely

Engineering and PM Lessons: 1. Deadline pressure is the greatest enemy of risk management — the closer the deadline, the more vigilantly risk protocols must be enforced, not relaxed 2. Known equipment deficiencies must be formally entered into the risk register — “we’ll fix it next month” is not a risk response 3. Public safety in and around construction exclusion zones is non-negotiable — it cannot be traded against schedule 4. Risk culture is built over years and destroyed in days — senior project leadership must model expected safety behaviour at peak pressure moments, not only in routine periods 5. “We have always done it this way without incident” is cognitive bias (normalisation of deviance), not a risk assessment

Discussion Questions: 1. Which of the six risk response strategies should SBG have applied to the crane stability risk during a known storm event at an occupied site? 2. What specific trigger events and thresholds should have been included in the risk register to prevent this incident? 3. How does schedule pressure influence risk decision-making on construction projects in the Arab world?

Sources: Al-Harthi and Al-Ghamdi (2016); MEED Projects (2016)

Caution✏️ Exercise 6.4 — Risk Response Plan

Scenario: You are managing the construction of a 200 MW concentrated solar power (CSP) plant in Wadi Rum desert, Jordan (8 parabolic trough field modules, each 1.2 km long, covering a total of 480 hectares, in ambient temperatures reaching 48°C with severe seasonal sandstorms between February and April).

The following risk has been identified and rated as High (4×4 = 16):

“A severe sandstorm (khamsin) damages mirror assemblies during construction, contaminating the heat transfer fluid system and causing significant schedule delay and equipment replacement cost.”

Develop a comprehensive Risk Response Plan for this risk:

  1. Justify why this is rated High (not Extreme or Medium)
  2. Evaluate ALL four threat response strategies (Avoid, Transfer, Mitigate, Accept) — recommend which to use and why
  3. Develop specific response actions with a named owner and deadline
  4. Define 3 trigger events that would activate the response
  5. Estimate the residual risk score after your response is implemented
  6. Identify the secondary risks created by your chosen response strategy

12.7 Project Audit

A project audit is a formal, independent assessment of a project conducted at a scheduled point during execution — not just at the end. The goal is to evaluate whether the project is performing as planned, identify corrective actions, and inform a formal go / no-go / restructure decision.

Note📖 Definition — Project Audit

A project audit is a structured, documented review of a project’s performance, processes, and compliance against its approved plan, governance requirements, and applicable standards. It is conducted by an independent reviewer or audit team — not the project team itself.

“The audit examines the reasons for success or failure and, more importantly, extracts lessons that can be applied to improve the management of future projects.” — Meredith & Mantel, Project Management: A Managerial Approach

12.7.1 Types of Project Audit

Types of project audit
Audit Type Trigger Focus
In-process Audit Scheduled (e.g., end of each phase) Is the project on track? Will it meet objectives?
Pre-completion Audit Near project end Are all deliverables complete? Is handover ready?
Termination Audit When cancellation is being considered Should the project continue, restructure, or terminate?
Post-project Audit 6–12 months after close Did the project deliver the expected benefits?

12.7.2 The Audit Process

12.7.3 What the Audit Examines

A comprehensive project audit reviews six key dimensions:

  • Scope & Deliverables
  • Schedule Performance
  • Cost Performance
  • Risk & Issues
  • Quality & Compliance
  • Team & Stakeholders
  • Are all contracted deliverables on track to be completed?
  • Is there uncontrolled scope growth (scope creep)?
  • Are changes being managed through the formal change control process?
  • Have any requirements been missed or misunderstood?
  • What is the current schedule variance (SV) and Schedule Performance Index (SPI)?
  • Are critical path activities on track?
  • Have schedule risks materialised? With what impact?
  • Is the forecast completion date still achievable?
  • What is the current cost variance (CV) and Cost Performance Index (CPI)?
  • Is the project tracking to the approved budget?
  • Are the contingency reserves adequate for remaining risks?
  • Forecast at Completion (FAC) vs. Budget at Completion (BAC)?
  • Are all identified risks being actively managed?
  • Have new risks emerged since last review?
  • Are issues being resolved in a timely manner?
  • Risk register current and updated?
  • Are quality assurance processes being followed?
  • How many non-conformances have been raised? Closed?
  • Are all regulatory and permit requirements being met?
  • Testing and inspection results within specification?
  • Is the project team adequately resourced?
  • Are key stakeholders engaged and informed?
  • Are there emerging interpersonal or organisational conflicts?
  • Is the PM receiving appropriate sponsor support?

12.7.4 The Audit Report

The audit report is the formal output delivered to the project sponsor and steering committee. It must be honest, evidence-based, and actionable.

Note📖 Audit Report Structure
Section Contents
Executive Summary Overall assessment (Green / Amber / Red), key findings, recommended decision
Scope Assessment Deliverables status, change control compliance, scope creep findings
Schedule Assessment Current SPI, critical path status, revised forecast completion date
Cost Assessment Current CPI, remaining budget adequacy, revised Estimate at Completion
Risk Assessment Top risks, residual risk profile, adequacy of contingency
Quality Findings NCR status, compliance status, test results summary
Findings & Issues Detailed list with owner and due date
Recommendations Specific, actionable corrective measures
Decision Required Continue as-is / Restructure / Escalate / Terminate

12.7.5 Project Health Traffic Light

Audits typically conclude with a traffic light assessment:

Project health traffic light ratings
Rating Meaning Typical Action
🟢 GREEN On track — no critical issues Continue, monitor normally
🟡 AMBER Issues identified — risk of failure without intervention PM to prepare corrective action plan within 2 weeks
🔴 RED Significant deviation — project objective at serious risk Sponsor intervention required; recovery plan or restructure
⬛ BLACK Unrecoverable — recommend formal termination Steering committee to decide on orderly close-down

## Quantitative Risk Analysis

**Quantitative Risk Analysis** assigns numerical values to risks, allowing financial exposure and schedule uncertainty to be modelled.

### Expected Monetary Value (EMV)

$$EMV = Probability \times Impact\ (\$)$$

**Example Risk Register with EMV:**

| Risk ID | Risk Description | Probability | Impact ($) | EMV ($) | Response |
|---------|----------------|------------|-----------|---------|---------|
| R-001 | Acid sulphate soils discovered | 30% | $350,000 | **$105,000** | Mitigation plan |
| R-002 | Steel supplier delays 6 weeks | 25% | $180,000 | **$45,000** | Alternative supplier |
| R-003 | Client scope additions | 60% | $120,000 | **$72,000** | Change control |
| R-004 | Regulatory approval extended | 20% | $250,000 | **$50,000** | Early pre-lodgement |
| R-005 | Favourable ground conditions | 40% | -$80,000 | **-$32,000** | Exploit opportunity |
| | | | **Total EMV** | **$240,000** | |

*The total EMV of $240,000 should be included in the project contingency fund.*

### For Opportunities (Positive Risks)

| Strategy | Description | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| **Exploit** | Ensure the opportunity definitely occurs | Assign best team to activity with potential for cost saving |
| **Enhance** | Increase probability or impact | Pre-order materials to benefit from good pricing |
| **Share** | Partner with another party to capture the opportunity | Joint venture to access larger market |
| **Accept** | Take advantage if it occurs without actively pursuing | Will take early completion bonus if it happens naturally |

## Case Study — Mecca Crane Collapse 2015: The Cost of Ignored Risk {.unnumbered}

::: {.callout-important icon="false"}
## 🏭 Case Study: Masjid al-Haram Crane Collapse — Risk Management Under Deadline Pressure

**Background:**  
On 11 September 2015 — just **11 days before Hajj** was due to begin — a Liebherr LR 11350 crane collapsed at the **Masjid al-Haram (Grand Mosque of Mecca)** construction site during a storm. The crane fell into the mosque's northern courtyard, directly onto pilgrims performing prayers. The collapse killed **111 people** and injured 394 others.

Saudi Binladin Group (SBG), one of the world's largest construction contractors and the Kingdom's most prestigious builder, was managing the largest ongoing expansion of Islam's holiest site.

**Risk Management Failures:**

| Risk Factor | What Should Have Happened | What Actually Happened |
|-------------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Crane securing protocol | Cranes must be secured in travel (free-slew) mode before storms; standards require de-rigging when wind exceeds 72 km/h | Wind reached 83 km/h; the crane remained rigged and unsecured in travel mode |
| Meteorological monitoring | 48-hour advance weather monitoring with automatic crane protocols triggered at defined wind thresholds | A weather alert was issued but not formally communicated to crane operators |
| Public exclusion zones | Live construction adjacent to an occupied worship space — an extraordinary risk context requiring enhanced protocols | No enhanced exclusion zone protocol existed for public prayer areas below active cranes during extreme weather |
| Equipment condition | All cranes subject to regular third-party certification | Investigation indicated the crane's luffing jib pin was in a degraded condition — a known maintenance concern |
| Risk culture | Supervisors must have authority to halt work for any safety concern | The 11-day countdown to Hajj created "normalisation of deviance" — routine shortcuts had accumulated over weeks without incident |

**The Normalisation of Deviance:**  
SBG had worked under intense Hajj-season deadline pressure for decades without a major fatal incident. Shortcuts that deviated from formal safety protocols had become routine because "nothing bad had happened before." The 2015 collapse demonstrates that statistical tolerance of risk does not reduce the underlying probability of catastrophe — it merely delays it.

**Financial and Operational Consequences:**
- Criminal investigation of SBG management; 13 individuals charged
- Following the crane collapse and a separate Mina pilgrimage stampede (24 September 2015), SBG was **suspended from all Saudi government contracts** by end of 2015
- SBG had revenues exceeding **$5 billion/year** — the suspension was an existential business event
- Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 subsequently restructured the Haramain and Haram construction contracting model entirely

**Engineering and PM Lessons:**
1. **Deadline pressure is the greatest enemy of risk management** — the closer the deadline, the more vigilantly risk protocols must be enforced, not relaxed
2. **Known equipment deficiencies must be formally entered into the risk register** — "we'll fix it next month" is not a risk response
3. **Public safety in and around construction exclusion zones is non-negotiable** — it cannot be traded against schedule
4. **Risk culture is built over years and destroyed in days** — senior project leadership must model expected safety behaviour at peak pressure moments, not only in routine periods
5. **"We have always done it this way without incident"** is cognitive bias (normalisation of deviance), not a risk assessment

**Discussion Questions:**
1. Which of the six risk response strategies should SBG have applied to the crane stability risk during a known storm event at an occupied site?
2. What specific trigger events and thresholds should have been included in the risk register to prevent this incident?
3. How does schedule pressure influence risk decision-making on construction projects in the Arab world?

*Sources: @mecca2015crane; @arab2016construction*
:::

## Project Audit

A **project audit** is a formal, independent assessment of a project conducted at a scheduled point during execution — not just at the end. The goal is to evaluate whether the project is performing as planned, identify corrective actions, and inform a formal **go / no-go / restructure** decision.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Definition — Project Audit

A **project audit** is a structured, documented review of a project's performance, processes, and compliance against its approved plan, governance requirements, and applicable standards. It is conducted by an independent reviewer or audit team — not the project team itself.

> *"The audit examines the reasons for success or failure and, more importantly, extracts lessons that can be applied to improve the management of future projects."*
> — Meredith & Mantel, *Project Management: A Managerial Approach*
:::

---

### Types of Project Audit

| Audit Type | Trigger | Focus |
|-----------|---------|-------|
| **In-process Audit** | Scheduled (e.g., end of each phase) | Is the project on track? Will it meet objectives? |
| **Pre-completion Audit** | Near project end | Are all deliverables complete? Is handover ready? |
| **Termination Audit** | When cancellation is being considered | Should the project continue, restructure, or terminate? |
| **Post-project Audit** | 6–12 months after close | Did the project deliver the expected benefits? |

### What the Audit Examines

A comprehensive project audit reviews six key dimensions:

::: {.panel-tabset}

## Scope & Deliverables
- Are all contracted deliverables on track to be completed?
- Is there uncontrolled scope growth (scope creep)?
- Are changes being managed through the formal change control process?
- Have any requirements been missed or misunderstood?

## Schedule Performance
- What is the current schedule variance (SV) and Schedule Performance Index (SPI)?
- Are critical path activities on track?
- Have schedule risks materialised? With what impact?
- Is the forecast completion date still achievable?

## Cost Performance
- What is the current cost variance (CV) and Cost Performance Index (CPI)?
- Is the project tracking to the approved budget?
- Are the contingency reserves adequate for remaining risks?
- Forecast at Completion (FAC) vs. Budget at Completion (BAC)?

## Risk & Issues
- Are all identified risks being actively managed?
- Have new risks emerged since last review?
- Are issues being resolved in a timely manner?
- Risk register current and updated?

## Quality & Compliance
- Are quality assurance processes being followed?
- How many non-conformances have been raised? Closed?
- Are all regulatory and permit requirements being met?
- Testing and inspection results within specification?

## Team & Stakeholders
- Is the project team adequately resourced?
- Are key stakeholders engaged and informed?
- Are there emerging interpersonal or organisational conflicts?
- Is the PM receiving appropriate sponsor support?

:::

---

### The Audit Report

The audit report is the formal output delivered to the project sponsor and steering committee. It must be honest, evidence-based, and actionable.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Audit Report Structure

| Section | Contents |
|---------|---------|
| **Executive Summary** | Overall assessment (Green / Amber / Red), key findings, recommended decision |
| **Scope Assessment** | Deliverables status, change control compliance, scope creep findings |
| **Schedule Assessment** | Current SPI, critical path status, revised forecast completion date |
| **Cost Assessment** | Current CPI, remaining budget adequacy, revised Estimate at Completion |
| **Risk Assessment** | Top risks, residual risk profile, adequacy of contingency |
| **Quality Findings** | NCR status, compliance status, test results summary |
| **Findings & Issues** | Detailed list with owner and due date |
| **Recommendations** | Specific, actionable corrective measures |
| **Decision Required** | Continue as-is / Restructure / Escalate / Terminate |
:::

---

### Project Health Traffic Light

Audits typically conclude with a **traffic light** assessment:

| Rating | Meaning | Typical Action |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| 🟢 **GREEN** | On track — no critical issues | Continue, monitor normally |
| 🟡 **AMBER** | Issues identified — risk of failure without intervention | PM to prepare corrective action plan within 2 weeks |
| 🔴 **RED** | Significant deviation — project objective at serious risk | Sponsor intervention required; recovery plan or restructure |
| ⬛ **BLACK** | Unrecoverable — recommend formal termination | Steering committee to decide on orderly close-down |
