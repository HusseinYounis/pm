Chapter 3 · Foundations

The Project Management Process Groups

A systematic walkthrough of the five process groups — from initiating through closing — applied to an engineering case study.

Learning Outcomes

Tip🎯 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Describe each of the five project management process groups in detail
  2. Map the process groups to the ten PMBOK knowledge areas
  3. Explain the key inputs, tools, and outputs of each process group
  4. Apply all five process groups to an engineering project scenario
  5. Develop a PM methodology appropriate for an engineering organisation

4.1 Overview of the Process Groups

The PMI defines five process groups that organise all project management activities. These are not sequential phases — they overlap and interact throughout the project:

Note📖 Key Concept — Process Groups vs. Phases

Process Groups are management activities — they happen in every phase. Phases are divisions of work specific to the project type.

In a construction project:

  • The Design Phase involves initiating (approve the design scope), planning (design schedule), executing (produce drawings), monitoring (design reviews), and closing (design freeze sign-off).
  • The Construction Phase then repeats all five process groups for the build work.

4.1.1 Level of Activity Across Process Groups

The amount of effort varies across the process groups. Planning and executing overlap significantly, with monitoring & controlling running continuously:

4.2 The Initiating Process Group

The Initiating process group authorises the project (or a new phase) and provides the high-level direction.

4.2.1 Key Activities

Initiating process group activities
Activity Description Key Output
Develop Business Case Justify the project — why is it needed? What’s the expected return? Business case document
Develop Project Charter Formally authorise the project; name the PM; define high-level scope, budget, timeline Project charter
Identify Stakeholders Determine who is affected by or can influence the project Stakeholder register
Note📖 Key Concept — The Project Charter

The project charter is the document that formally authorises the project and gives the PM the authority to apply organisational resources. Without a signed charter, the project does not officially exist.

Key elements: project purpose, measurable objectives, high-level requirements, summary budget, milestone schedule, PM name and authority level, sponsor name and approval.

4.3 The Planning Process Group

Planning is the largest process group, encompassing 24 of the 49 PMBOK processes. It defines what needs to be done, how, when, and by whom.

4.3.1 Key Activities

Important⚠️ Common Mistake — Rushing to Execute

Many engineers are eager to “start building” and treat planning as bureaucracy. But poor planning is the #1 cause of project failure. The rule of thumb: every hour spent planning saves 3–5 hours during execution.

4.3.2 The Project Management Plan

The Project Management Plan (PMP) is the master document that integrates all subsidiary plans:

Components of the integrated Project Management Plan
Subsidiary Plan Defines
Scope Management Plan How scope will be defined, validated, and controlled
Schedule Management Plan How the schedule will be developed, managed, and controlled
Cost Management Plan How costs will be estimated, budgeted, and controlled
Quality Management Plan Quality standards and how they will be assured and controlled
Resource Management Plan How resources will be identified, acquired, and managed
Communications Management Plan Who needs what information, when, and in what format
Risk Management Plan How risks will be identified, analysed, and responded to
Procurement Management Plan What to procure, how, and from whom
Stakeholder Engagement Plan How stakeholders will be engaged throughout the project

4.3.3 The Three Baselines

The PMP includes three baselines that serve as the reference points for measuring project performance:

  1. Scope Baseline = Scope Statement + WBS + WBS Dictionary
  2. Schedule Baseline = Approved project schedule
  3. Cost Baseline = Time-phased project budget

Changes to any baseline require formal change control — you cannot simply move the goalposts.

4.4 The Executing Process Group

Executing is where the actual work gets done. The PM’s focus shifts from planning to coordinating resources, managing the team, and ensuring quality.

4.4.1 Key Activities

Executing process group activities
Activity Description
Direct and Manage Project Work Lead the team in producing deliverables according to the plan
Manage Project Knowledge Capture and share lessons learned and organisational knowledge
Manage Quality Perform quality assurance — audit processes and standards compliance
Acquire Resources Obtain the people, equipment, and materials needed
Develop Team Build team competence and cohesion (training, team-building)
Manage Team Track performance, resolve conflicts, provide feedback
Manage Communications Distribute information per the communications plan
Implement Risk Responses Execute the planned risk response strategies
Conduct Procurements Select sellers, negotiate contracts, issue purchase orders
Manage Stakeholder Engagement Work with stakeholders to meet their needs and address issues
Tip💡 Practical Tip — Where Time and Money Are Spent

The executing process group consumes the most time and the most budget of any process group — typically 60–80% of total project resources. Effective planning and monitoring make this investment efficient; poor planning wastes it.

4.5 The Monitoring and Controlling Process Group

This process group runs continuously throughout the project. It tracks performance against the plan and takes corrective action when deviations occur.

4.5.1 Key Activities

Activity Knowledge Area
Monitor and Control Project Work Integration
Perform Integrated Change Control Integration
Validate Scope Scope
Control Scope Scope
Control Schedule Schedule
Control Costs Cost
Control Quality Quality
Control Resources Resources
Monitor Communications Communications
Monitor Risks Risk
Control Procurements Procurement
Monitor Stakeholder Engagement Stakeholder
Note📖 Key Concept — Integrated Change Control

All changes to project baselines (scope, schedule, cost) must go through a formal change control process:

  1. Request — Document the change request (CR)
  2. Evaluate — Assess the impact on scope, schedule, cost, quality, and risk
  3. Decide — Change Control Board (CCB) approves, defers, or rejects
  4. Implement — If approved, update the plan and baselines
  5. Communicate — Notify all affected stakeholders

Uncontrolled changes are scope creep — the silent killer of projects.

4.5.2 Configuration Management

Configuration Management ensures that the descriptions of project products and deliverables are correct, complete, and consistent at all times — from initial definition through to final handover.

Note📖 What is Configuration Management?

While Integrated Change Control asks: “Should this change be approved?”, Configuration Management asks: “Is what we are building still accurately described in our documents?”

It tracks and controls the physical and functional characteristics of project deliverables, ensuring that: - Product specifications match what is actually being built - All documentation reflects the latest approved version - No unauthorised changes have been made to the product description

Activity Description
Configuration Identification Define and document the characteristics of each deliverable
Configuration Control Ensure product descriptions are correct and complete; prevent unauthorised changes
Configuration Status Accounting Record and report the status of all configurations
Configuration Audit Verify that the deliverable matches its documented specification
Important⚠️ Exam Tip — Configuration Management vs. Integrated Change Control
Configuration Management Integrated Change Control
Focus Product descriptions & specifications Change requests to baselines
Question it answers Are the product descriptions correct and complete? Should this change be approved?
Managed by Configuration Manager / PM Change Control Board (CCB)

“To ensure that the description of project products are correct and complete” → Configuration Management ✅

4.6 The Closing Process Group

Closing formalises acceptance of the project’s deliverables and brings the project (or phase) to an orderly end.

4.6.1 Key Activities

Important⚠️ Common Mistake — Skipping Closure

Many projects drift to an end without formal closure. This means:

  • No lessons learned captured → same mistakes repeated
  • No formal acceptance → disputes about what was delivered
  • Resources not released → they remain allocated but idle
  • Contracts not closed → lingering financial liabilities

Note📖 Key Concept — The Matrix as a Roadmap

This matrix is the roadmap of project management. When you’re unsure what to do next on a project, find your current process group and knowledge area in the matrix — the cell tells you what process to perform.

Notice that:

  • Planning has the most processes (24 of 49)
  • Integration is the only knowledge area that spans all five process groups
  • Initiating and Closing are the smallest but arguably the most neglected

4.8 Developing a PM Methodology

An organisation’s PM methodology defines the standard approach to managing projects. It adapts the generic PMBOK framework to the organisation’s specific context.

4.8.1 Elements of an Engineering PM Methodology

Tip💡 Practical Tip — Tailoring

No methodology should be applied blindly. The PMBOK 7th Edition emphasises tailoring — adapting processes, tools, and documentation to the project’s size, complexity, and risk level. A $10M highway widening does not need the same level of documentation as a $2B metro system.

Case Study — Al-Maktoum International Airport Expansion, Dubai

Caution🏗️ Case Study — Al-Maktoum International Airport Expansion

Al-Maktoum International Airport (DWC) in Dubai South is planned to become the world’s largest airport, with an ultimate capacity of 260 million passengers per year. The expansion programme illustrates all five process groups in action.

4.8.2 Project Overview

Attribute Detail
Location Dubai South (Jebel Ali), UAE
Estimated cost $35+ billion (full build-out)
Phases Multi-phase over 20+ years
Client Dubai Aviation Engineering Projects (DAEP)
Key challenge Phased construction while maintaining existing operations

4.8.3 Process Groups in Action

  • 🚀 Initiating
  • 📋 Planning
  • ⚙️ Executing
  • 📊 Monitoring & Controlling
  • 🏁 Closing
  • Strategic alignment: Part of Dubai’s vision to be the world’s aviation hub, aligning with Dubai 2040 Urban Master Plan
  • Business case: Forecast demand exceeding Dubai International Airport’s (DXB) 100M passenger capacity
  • Project charter: Defined scope as a phased expansion — runway, terminals, cargo, ground transportation
  • Stakeholder identification: UAE General Civil Aviation Authority, airlines, Dubai Tourism, neighbouring Expo 2020/District 2040 developments, 200+ airline customers
  • Scope: Full WBS developed for each phase — airside (runways, taxiways, ATC tower), landside (terminals, parking, access roads), systems (baggage, security, IT)
  • Schedule: Primavera P6 master schedule with 50,000+ activities; critical path through terminal foundations and runway construction
  • Cost: Class 3 estimate progressing to Class 1; Earned Value Management mandated on all packages
  • Quality: ITP covering all structural, MEP, and ICT systems; BIM Level 2 mandated
  • Risk: 500+ risks in the register; key risks include ground conditions (sabkha soil), extreme heat (50°C+), supply chain disruptions, interface conflicts between packages
  • Team management: Peak workforce estimated at 60,000+ workers from 40+ nationalities
  • Procurement: Multiple design-build packages awarded to international joint ventures
  • Quality assurance: Third-party inspection agencies; BIM clash detection before every pour
  • Communications: Weekly dashboards to DAEP leadership; monthly reports to Dubai Executive Council
  • Stakeholder engagement: Regular coordination with existing DWC operations to minimise disruption
  • Earned Value: Monthly EVM reporting (CPI, SPI) at programme, package, and activity level
  • Change control: Programme Change Board meets bi-weekly; all changes assessed for impact on schedule, cost, and interfaces
  • Schedule monitoring: 4-week lookahead schedules; delay analysis using Time Impact Analysis (TIA) method
  • Risk monitoring: Monthly risk reviews; risks that materialise become issues tracked in a separate register
  • Scope control: BIM model frozen at stage gates; any post-freeze changes require formal variation order
  • Phased handover: Each terminal zone goes through Testing & Commissioning (T&C), Operational Readiness (ORAT), and formal handover
  • Lessons learned: Captured at the end of each phase; fed into the planning of subsequent phases
  • Document turnover: As-built BIM models, O&M manuals, and warranty certificates collected per handover zone
  • Benefits realisation: Passenger capacity and airline satisfaction measured against business case projections

4.8.4 Lessons for Process Groups

  1. All five process groups operate in every phase — initiation happens again when a new terminal phase is authorised; planning is continuous; monitoring never stops
  2. The planning process group is the backbone — 50,000+ scheduled activities require meticulous planning to coordinate
  3. Monitoring & controlling prevents surprises — EVM and schedule analysis catch deviations early
  4. Closing is critical in phased projects — each phase must be formally closed and handed over before the next can fully begin
  5. Integration is the PM’s core job — with hundreds of interfaces between packages, the programme management team’s primary role is integration

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 Key Concept — Integrated Change Control

**All changes** to project baselines (scope, schedule, cost) must go through a formal **change control process**:

1. **Request** — Document the change request (CR)
2. **Evaluate** — Assess the impact on scope, schedule, cost, quality, and risk
3. **Decide** — Change Control Board (CCB) approves, defers, or rejects
4. **Implement** — If approved, update the plan and baselines
5. **Communicate** — Notify all affected stakeholders

Uncontrolled changes are **scope creep** — the silent killer of projects.
:::

### Configuration Management

**Configuration Management** ensures that the descriptions of project products and deliverables are **correct, complete, and consistent** at all times — from initial definition through to final handover.

::: {.callout-note icon="false"}
## 📖 What is Configuration Management?

While **Integrated Change Control** asks: *"Should this change be approved?"*,
**Configuration Management** asks: *"Is what we are building still accurately described in our documents?"*

It tracks and controls the **physical and functional characteristics** of project deliverables, ensuring that:
- Product specifications match what is actually being built
- All documentation reflects the latest approved version
- No unauthorised changes have been made to the product description
:::

| Activity | Description |
|---|---|
| **Configuration Identification** | Define and document the characteristics of each deliverable |
| **Configuration Control** | Ensure product descriptions are correct and complete; prevent unauthorised changes |
| **Configuration Status Accounting** | Record and report the status of all configurations |
| **Configuration Audit** | Verify that the deliverable matches its documented specification |

::: {.callout-important icon="false"}
## ⚠️ Exam Tip — Configuration Management vs. Integrated Change Control

| | Configuration Management | Integrated Change Control |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | Product descriptions & specifications | Change requests to baselines |
| **Question it answers** | Are the product descriptions correct and complete? | Should this change be approved? |
| **Managed by** | Configuration Manager / PM | Change Control Board (CCB) |

> *"To ensure that the description of project products are correct and complete"* → **Configuration Management** ✅
:::

## ⚠️ Common Mistake — Skipping Closure

Many projects drift to an end without formal closure. This means:

- No lessons learned captured → same mistakes repeated
- No formal acceptance → disputes about what was delivered
- Resources not released → they remain allocated but idle
- Contracts not closed → lingering financial liabilities
:::

## Case Study — Al-Maktoum International Airport Expansion, Dubai {.unnumbered}

::: {.callout-caution icon="false"}
## 🏗️ Case Study — Al-Maktoum International Airport Expansion

**Al-Maktoum International Airport** (DWC) in Dubai South is planned to become the world's largest airport, with an ultimate capacity of 260 million passengers per year. The expansion programme illustrates all five process groups in action.

### Project Overview

| Attribute | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Location** | Dubai South (Jebel Ali), UAE |
| **Estimated cost** | $35+ billion (full build-out) |
| **Phases** | Multi-phase over 20+ years |
| **Client** | Dubai Aviation Engineering Projects (DAEP) |
| **Key challenge** | Phased construction while maintaining existing operations |

### Process Groups in Action

::: {.panel-tabset}

#### 🚀 Initiating

- **Strategic alignment:** Part of Dubai's vision to be the world's aviation hub, aligning with Dubai 2040 Urban Master Plan
- **Business case:** Forecast demand exceeding Dubai International Airport's (DXB) 100M passenger capacity
- **Project charter:** Defined scope as a phased expansion — runway, terminals, cargo, ground transportation
- **Stakeholder identification:** UAE General Civil Aviation Authority, airlines, Dubai Tourism, neighbouring Expo 2020/District 2040 developments, 200+ airline customers

#### 📋 Planning

- **Scope:** Full WBS developed for each phase — airside (runways, taxiways, ATC tower), landside (terminals, parking, access roads), systems (baggage, security, IT)
- **Schedule:** Primavera P6 master schedule with 50,000+ activities; critical path through terminal foundations and runway construction
- **Cost:** Class 3 estimate progressing to Class 1; Earned Value Management mandated on all packages
- **Quality:** ITP covering all structural, MEP, and ICT systems; BIM Level 2 mandated
- **Risk:** 500+ risks in the register; key risks include ground conditions (sabkha soil), extreme heat (50°C+), supply chain disruptions, interface conflicts between packages

#### ⚙️ Executing

- **Team management:** Peak workforce estimated at 60,000+ workers from 40+ nationalities
- **Procurement:** Multiple design-build packages awarded to international joint ventures
- **Quality assurance:** Third-party inspection agencies; BIM clash detection before every pour
- **Communications:** Weekly dashboards to DAEP leadership; monthly reports to Dubai Executive Council
- **Stakeholder engagement:** Regular coordination with existing DWC operations to minimise disruption

#### 📊 Monitoring & Controlling

- **Earned Value:** Monthly EVM reporting (CPI, SPI) at programme, package, and activity level
- **Change control:** Programme Change Board meets bi-weekly; all changes assessed for impact on schedule, cost, and interfaces
- **Schedule monitoring:** 4-week lookahead schedules; delay analysis using Time Impact Analysis (TIA) method
- **Risk monitoring:** Monthly risk reviews; risks that materialise become issues tracked in a separate register
- **Scope control:** BIM model frozen at stage gates; any post-freeze changes require formal variation order

#### 🏁 Closing

- **Phased handover:** Each terminal zone goes through Testing & Commissioning (T&C), Operational Readiness (ORAT), and formal handover
- **Lessons learned:** Captured at the end of each phase; fed into the planning of subsequent phases
- **Document turnover:** As-built BIM models, O&M manuals, and warranty certificates collected per handover zone
- **Benefits realisation:** Passenger capacity and airline satisfaction measured against business case projections

:::

### Lessons for Process Groups

1. **All five process groups operate in every phase** — initiation happens again when a new terminal phase is authorised; planning is continuous; monitoring never stops
2. **The planning process group is the backbone** — 50,000+ scheduled activities require meticulous planning to coordinate
3. **Monitoring & controlling prevents surprises** — EVM and schedule analysis catch deviations early
4. **Closing is critical in phased projects** — each phase must be formally closed and handed over before the next can fully begin
5. **Integration is the PM's core job** — with hundreds of interfaces between packages, the programme management team's primary role is integration

:::

## 📝 Chapter Summary

In this chapter you learned that:

- The five **process groups** — Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, and Closing — organise all PM activities
- Process groups **overlap and repeat** within each project phase; they are not a once-through sequence
- **Planning** is the largest process group with 24 processes; **Integration** is the only knowledge area spanning all five groups
- The **Process Group × Knowledge Area matrix** is the roadmap of project management — 49 processes organised across 10 knowledge areas
- **Integrated change control** protects the project baselines from uncontrolled scope creep
- **Closing** is essential — formal acceptance, lessons learned, and document archival prevent future disputes and repeated mistakes
- Organisations should develop a **PM methodology** tailored to their specific context, adapting generic best practices to their industry and project types
